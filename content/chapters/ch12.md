# XII. The Flickering Bridge
*In which a bridge flickers, a library keeps a hole, and a scholar is handed a stone*

You see the towers first, then the trees among the towers, and then you understand that the city and the forest have an arrangement older than either's pride: Silverymoon does not so much stand on the north bank of the Rauvin as grow there. The party came up the south road in the shine after the rain, and the Gem of the North did what it has done to travelers for four hundred years, which is make them briefly forget what they came about.

The Gem of the North earns the name the tedious way, by being all the things the epithets claim. No quarter here belongs to a race: humans and elves and dwarves keep the same streets, and the streets themselves seem grown as much as built — stonework that curves the way living things curve, towers wound through with trees older than the towers, hanging gardens, crystal lamps that light themselves at dusk. Over all of it lies the Mythal: the city's ancient warding, a permanence in the air more felt than seen, said to turn away scrying and things of true evil alike — and visible, if you know to look, in small graces, like the way a dropped scarf settles a half-beat too slowly. The Knights in Silver keep the walls and the Spellguard keeps the sky, and both answer to the High Marshal, Methrammar Aerasumé — for the city's famous ruler is famously gone. For centuries @[Silverymoon](silverymoon) was High Lady @[Alustriel Silverhand](alustriel)'s, and her name is still said here in the tone reserved for beloved absent things; the party had heard that name once already, through a mask, at Boar's Rest, and hearing it again over every second shop-door gave them the particular vertigo of a small world.

Then they saw the bridge. The Moonbridge is the city's signature and its oldest boast — an arc of solid moonlight flung across the river, no stone in it anywhere, bearing carts and lovers and funeral processions with the same unbothered grace since before anyone living was born. It glowed as advertised. And as they watched, it flickered — once, the way a lamp flickers when the oil runs low — a single skipped heartbeat of light, and the traffic upon it did not pause, and a fruit-seller by the gate went on stacking apples, and that, more than the flicker, was the thing that frightened them.

The city had built scaffolds of explanation around its bridge the way Triboar had built festival booths around its dread. Seasonal attunement, said the official notices, posted in three scripts. A recalibration of the mythal, said the wizards' bulletin, which is the Silverymoon way of saying we are looking into it in a city where looking into it is a profession. The people repeated these comforts to each other in shop doorways with the exact cadence the party had heard once before, in another town, in autumn, and hoped never to hear again. It is not that anyone was lying. It is that management had been mistaken for safety, which is the oldest civic error there is.

And there was a legation in the city — an embassy in all but treaty: envoys, clerks, a fine rented house, the whole machinery by which a young state introduces itself to an old one. Banners of deep gray and silver hung from a fine house in the Northbank quarter, and clerks came and went with the unhurried step of people whose business is measured in years. The new city, the salons called it, the young polity in the far north-east — out past Sundabar and the last confident maps, where the land forgets its names toward dead Ascore and the great desert's edge that traders had begun to mention the way one mentions weather in another country. It had a name, and the name was starting to circulate on Silverymoon's best tongues: @[Fundament](fundament). It sounded like a promise about floors. Its officials, the salons noted with delight or unease depending on the wine, went by virtues instead of names — an affectation of the new city's civil service, apparently, and either charming or sinister, and nobody could decide which. The party heard the word for the first time across a crowded room — someone bright and laughing, being introduced: @[Clarity](clarity), of the legation — and around their table four cups stopped at four different heights, because they had seen that word before, at the bottom of nothing, and it is one thing to know your enemy has signatures and another to watch a signature stand up, smooth her coat, and walk.

They saw her properly only for a moment, that first day, and across a street: a tiefling woman of middle years, dusk-skinned, horns ringed in plain silver, dressed with the exact restraint that costs the most — laughing at something a Silverymoon councilor had said, and making the councilor visibly taller by laughing. @[Every instinct](clarity) in the party leaned toward crossing the street. Every instinct that had kept them alive leaned harder the other way: you do not introduce yourself to a signature. You find out, first, what it signs. They kept walking — they had a card in hand and a scholar to spend it on — and the laughing went on behind them like weather.

They found Theron Veil under the bridge — not poetically; literally, down among the footings on the north bank, boots in the river-mud, holding a tuning fork against the oldest stonework and listening with his eyes shut — and it was he who knew them, before any of them could have picked his unmasked face from a crowd. Masks had been the whole grammar of Boar's Rest; names had been given truly, faces not at all; but there are limits to what a mask can do for a company that includes a seven-foot firbolg and a gnome with a violin, and the scholar hailed them from the mud by composition alone, delighted, waving the fork. Unmasked he proved a spare, ink-stained man with river-colored eyes and the complexion of someone who reads standing up in cold rooms. In truth he knew almost none of them — @[Amyl](amyl)'s name, the others' silhouettes — and it made no difference at all: his relief at the sight of them was terrible to watch, the relief of a man who had spent a month crossing names off a private list and finally, finally got to add. What they shared outweighed what they knew of each other, and both parties leaned on it without shame.

He asked — quietly, the mud forgotten — what had been under the town. They told him what they could: the old colony in the deeps, the drowned architecture of the brain-things, and what the Sharrans had bolted into it. The scholar stood very still in the river-mud, vindicated and appalled in a single expression. “An illithid colony. Under Triboar. I wrote a paper once,” he said, “that no one read, proposing exactly — ” and then he stopped, because being right had never before cost him this much. @[Sov](sov), who forgot nothing, chose that moment to hand him the small black corner of altar-stone he had carried out of the mirror-cave a season ago — cold, faintly humming, wrong in the palm. Theron took it the way some men take relics. “Yes,” he said, to no question anyone had asked. “Leave this with me.”

His own work he showed them in a notebook furred with figures. “A bridge is a chord,” he told them. “Stone, span, mythal, moon — it sounds together or not at all. And something is detuning this one. A string a week. Deliberate as a bookkeeper. I keep asking who profits from a city that stops trusting its own light, and I have not liked any of my answers.”

It was Theron who sent them to the Vault of the Sages — they had asked him, boots still in the river-mud, where a person looks a thing up in this city when the thing is old and unlucky, and he had laughed properly for the first time since Triboar and named the only possible answer — the great library of the North, a domed palace of shelves and lamplight on the city's western rise, where Harpers and scholars and half the continent's questions eventually wash up — armed with a note to a head archivist who, the note implied, owed him either money or friendship. The Vault took @[Aelendir](aelendir) the way a river takes rain. He had come, he claimed, merely to accompany @[Corvyn](corvyn)'s researches; within an hour there were librarians involved, and within two there was a small procession of them, because someone had pulled the chronicle of a winter court's revels from two hundred years ago and found in it a spring eladrin of unusual courtesy, performing, described to the life. Then a chronicle from a century before that. Then another. The same figure, the same courtesy, the same unchanged face across shelves that measured their distances in generations — the stacks verifying Aelendir the way sediment verifies a river. He received all this with the mild pleasure of a man being shown his own portrait. And then the head archivist, delighted, went to fetch the era that should have held the most of him — and came back slowly, without delight, holding nothing. There was a gap. Not a thinness: a gap, an era's shelf where his name simply ceased to occur, as if for some span of years every scribe on the continent had been paid by the silence. The archivist apologized the way professionals apologize for things that are not their fault and cannot be explained. Aelendir stood very still in front of the place where he wasn't, for longer than the librarians found comfortable, and when he turned away he was humming, almost silently, and did not appear to know it.

@[Sov](sov) used the librarians' distraction to ask his own question of the stacks, quietly, the way he asked everything: where a man might read about a @[Queen of ravens](the-raven-queen). The catalogs offered nothing worth the walk. But in a book he pulled half at random from a neighboring shelf — a dull treatise on northern mourning-knots, of all things — something was waiting between the pages: a black feather, pressed flat, old as the binding, and laid straight even there. No catalog note explained it. The page it marked showed a diagram of a common grief-knot, the kind tied at ten thousand ordinary funerals, and he stared at it for a long time and learned nothing, and kept the feather, and did not mention it to the librarians, on the reasoning — which he did not examine — that it had been left for him.

They were three days into the city before @[Sov](sov) said aloud what he had known since the first morning: the feathers had followed them upriver. One lay on the sill of a Kelemvorite chapel near the east gate, straight as a compass needle. Another waited at the edge of a pauper's funeral that Sov happened to pass — happened, the record insists, and the record is not sure it believes itself — where a priest with a tired, kind voice spoke the dead man's full name three times to a crowd of nobody, because a name spoken is a thing kept. Sov stood at the fence until it was over. He put the feather in his pocket with the other one. He was starting a collection the way a lock starts a collection of keys.

What unsettled him — what he did not say aloud — was that he had begun to read them. Lessons, he called them privately; compasses; and sometimes, worse, affirmations: a @[feather arriving after a choice](the-raven-queen), laid straight, as if something somewhere had reviewed the choice and found it correct. On his honest nights he admitted the readings were entirely his own. The feathers said nothing. They only arrived, and were exact, and seemed to approve — and he had begun to want the approval, which frightened him considerably more than the mystery did.
