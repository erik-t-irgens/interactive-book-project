# X. Harvest
*In which High Harvestide comes to Triboar, and the harvest comes for it*

@audio: triboar-hearth fade=6
The festival went ahead. Understand that before you judge it: there was nowhere to send two and a half thousand frightened people that was safer than the walls they already had, and @[Darathra Shendrel](darathra) had governed too long to believe you can evacuate a town into a wilderness on the strength of a dread. What she could do quietly, she did at dawn — the children and the old moved out toward the western farmsteads for the festival, wagons of them on the Gwaeron's Rest road before the bread was out of the ovens. The rest of Triboar hung its lanterns on the surviving posts, because the alternative was panic, and panic kills faster than almost anything the party had yet met.

They found the Lord Protector in the burned records office, standing in the char of her own institution with her sleeves rolled, and they made their last accounting together in that bitter-smelling room. She did not waste it on apology. She counted what was known — the traitor gone down, the raiders' orders, the rumble that had moved from beneath their feet to before their faces — and she paid out the night's one piece of grace like a quartermaster: @[Gorvach](gorvach) could not stay. The Tower was breached, the town's mood was a struck match, and the truce would not survive his being found; so she walked the party and the great orc up the Tower's winding heart to a room at its top that very few living people had seen, where a standing arch of pale stone held a stillness that was not empty. A portal, keyed and kept since before her office had a name: @[Moongleam Tower](moongleam), in Everlund, a @[Harper](the-harpers) hearth on the far side of the Evermoors. She sent Gorvach through it into the care of quiet people and the long road north, and the last thing he did on the Triboar side was look at @[Tirig](tirig) — the compass-look, one final time — and touch two fingers to his brow where the frost had lived. Then the arch took him. And the party stood in the highest room of the Tower knowing something they had not known an hour before: there was a door out of this town that did not use the roads.

@audio: undergrave-drone fade=5
They went down the sluice at mid-morning, while above them the festival tuned its fiddles. Wet stone; the grit of black ice in the boot-prints of dead raiders; and then the smugglers' brickwork gave out and the tunnels changed. The walls went smooth — bored, not built, curved like the inside of a throat, ringed at intervals with alcoves shaped for things that were not amphorae. Theron Veil had been righter than his own footnotes. Something had hollowed a colony under Triboar in an age no ledger reached, and the party walked its dead streets by torchlight: chambers like surgical theaters, a dry basin big enough to bathe a whale in, tool-marks that made the eye look away and count its fingers. Whatever had lived here was long gone. What had moved in after was not.

@[Gregory](gregory)'s small prints ran ahead of them the whole way, unhurried in the dust. @[Merris](merris)'s boots ran beside them for a time, then apart, taking a branching the party marked and did not take. And under everything, growing with every turn, the rumble — no longer a sound so much as a condition, the stone itself troubled — and through the spore-link, faint and constant, @[Amyl](amyl) and @[Corvyn](corvyn) felt the @[buried Presence](the-quiet-thing) the way one feels a housefire from the street: vast, near, terrified. And threaded through its terror, unmistakable now, a note neither of them had felt in it before. It was not alone down here. It had the boy, or the boy had it, and neither of them wanted what was coming.

@audio: glass-rise fade=6
They heard the liturgy before they saw the light — not the words, the cadence, that patient devout meter the party had been reading in notes signed by virtues since the season began. The tunnel opened onto a gallery, and the gallery onto the place the whole mystery had been walking toward: an amphitheater cut by the colony's old owners, rings of stone descending to a floor — and on the rings, arranged with terrible care, the dead. Months of the missing. The vanished refugees, the disappeared, the ones the town had stopped counting — laid out in circles like an audience, hundreds of them, a necrotic congregation banked around the stage. The air over them was thin in a way the party's souls felt before their skins did: this many dead, in this much dark, on this night of the year, had worn the veil to a membrane. And above the amphitheater's center, where a chandelier would hang in a kinder room, hung the stone.

The @[mooring stone](mooring-stones) of the wood had been the size of a heart. This was the size of a bell tower — a black monolith suspended in a lattice of chains that ran away into the dark in every direction like the rigging of a buried ship, its veins already lit, already drinking, a slow pulse running its length with the patience of something that knows what time it is. Beneath it, on the stage, the celebrants — two dozen in the gray-and-black, and at their center a senior of the faith with a voice like a closing door. And among the congregation of the standing, a woman in a gray cut almost to Twelve-issue, wearing an inquisitor's sash — and though not one of them had ever seen her face, they knew her the way you know a signature: @[Merris Thulane](merris), it could be no one else, and she did not look surprised to see them at all.

And the working already had a product. Low along the floor, wherever stone met stone, the seams of the amphitheater had begun to fill — hairlines of black glass, glossy and slow, welling up out of the joints of the world like blood finding the cracks in a hand. It did not pour and it did not spread. It rose, patiently, at the pace of the monolith's pulse, and where it rose the stone around it gave up its color, and the party understood without discussion that they were watching the rite's true output arriving ahead of schedule.

The celebrant had a sermon prepared. Knowing you, he would have gotten four words into it. The fight came down the amphitheater rings the way water comes down stairs, and it was vicious and it was uphill-into-them and the party won it — through the wretched arithmetic of two dozen zealots against seven people the Weave itself had opinions about — and none of it mattered in the way they wanted it to matter. The celebrant died with his hands still raised, not in surrender: in benediction, and what he said instead of begging was, @[You cannot stop a season](the-harvest). And the stone drank on, exactly as before, because the working had not needed its priests for hours. They were not conducting it. They were attending it.

@unlock: saeron.2
Two things came out of that fight. The first was on the celebrant's body: a note in the firm, unhurried hand the party knew better than some of their own — The field is gathered. Prepare the next. —Verity — folded around a page of instructions for a town the party had never heard of: methods, schedules, the placement of assurances, the pattern of quiet disappearances by quarter and by season. And it was @[Saeron](saeron) who went still over it. Whatever the page was to him, it was not new — it read on his face, the others said afterward, like a rhyme: some old verse of his own that this page had stolen the meter from, in different streets, under a different sky. He offered nothing, and no one asked. He folded the page into his robes with the care of a man handling his own past, and the party let him keep it, and the Cord Door had begun to open though none of them would have called it that yet.

The second thing was @[Merris](merris). She did not fight to the last; she was not that kind of believer. At the turn of the battle she stepped backward off the stage's far edge — to where the glass ran thickest, black and slow along the seams of the amphitheater floor — and she looked at the party with an expression they would spend a long time failing to file, because it was not defiance and it was not fear. It was relief. She stepped back into the rising glass the way one steps backward into a warm room, and it closed over her smiling, and the party understood they had just watched a fanatic choose her own altar. That is what they understood.

Below the amphitheater, the anchor-room. The chamber was the colony's deepest vault, and it was full — wall to wall and floor to unseen ceiling — with the thing the party had been feeling underfoot since a rat-hole a season ago. They never saw all of it. Nobody was ever meant to see all of it: a landscape of the @[buried Presence](the-quiet-thing), root-mass and dark tissue and slow luminous ropes of it running away into the earth, and clamped across it everywhere the Sharrans' couplings — collars of black glass, lattice-chains, sigil-pins driven in like harpoons, the whole harness of a captive engine. The rumble was its voice at rest. It was not at rest now. And in the center of the chamber, in a socket of pale light where all the couplings converged, stood @[Gregory](gregory) — barefoot, calm, his small hands at his sides, talking to it in the only language it trusted, which was his.

The smell arrived before understanding did: rot and musk, deep and organic, the smell of a forest floor magnified into weather. And understanding, when it came, was worse, because the chamber was not a room containing the thing — the room was partly the thing. Walls that looked like stone gave, faintly, underhand. Buttresses were limbs at rest. And up close, where the lantern light reached, the truth of its body resolved: a vast slow mass of once-things — leaf-mold and heartwood, the small architectures of rats, the long bones of men, root and antler and wing, all of it neither alive nor dead nor decaying exactly, but held in some third state between, participating in each other with a patience that was almost peace. It was not grotesque. That was the unsettling part. It looked, more than anything, like belonging.

The tentacles were the storm. When the party entered, the chamber defended what it held the only way the harness allowed — limbs of the Presence, lashed and driven by the couplings, rising out of the dark in waves, and the party fighting through them toward the boy at the eye: sever a coupling and a limb went slack and gentle; leave it and be swung at; and everywhere @[Amyl](amyl) and @[Corvyn](corvyn) feeling, through the spores, the truth their sword-arms had to ignore — that the thing attacking them was screaming, that every blow it landed was being wrung out of it, that they were fighting the harness and the harness was wearing a mountain.

I never wrote the version where you killed him. I started to, once — the version where the fastest way to still the engine is to close the channel, and the channel is a child — and I stopped, because some branches you prune before they can grow, and because I knew my table. You would have found the other way if it took you all night. Here is the other way, as I built it: Amyl and Corvyn, deep in the spore-link, reaching the Presence through Gregory — not commanding, reassuring — and Gregory relaying, both directions, the smallest ambassador in the history of embassies. The talk-down was three truths, passed hand to hand into the dark: You are not alone. This is not your fault. You are allowed to let go. And the @[Gravemind](the-quiet-thing) — there is no point withholding the name from you now — let go. It slipped the sheared couplings like a whale rolling out of a net, not free, not whole, but no longer entirely harnessed; the storm of limbs sank; and the boy in the socket of light swayed, and sat down abruptly on the stone, and began, at last, to cry like the child he had never quite been allowed to be. Amyl carried him out. The first person the party ever saved, saved twice, small against that great chest, gray to the elbows with the dust of a buried world.

And it changed the terms, and it did not change the outcome. Somewhere above them the sun had gone down on High Harvestide, and the stone tolled — not a sound; a pressure, felt in the teeth and the sternum and the fillings of the world — and every lit vein in it went from pulse to pour. Winning the room had widened the working's mercy by exactly as much as they had shorn from its harness. It had not touched the working. The season, as promised, could not be stopped.

---

Sound reached them first — wrong sound, festival sound, bells and voices where there should have been either battle or silence — and with it, word from above: the Tower had emptied, Darathra and the last of her people gone out into the streets, and in the crevasses between the paving stones of Triboar the black glass had begun to pool, quiet as rainwater, rising along the seams of the town exactly as it had risen along the seams of the amphitheater. There was nothing left to do below. The sound ushered them up.

They came up out of the earth into a festival. That is the part I could never have run with a straight face and can barely write with one: the lanterns were lit, the masks were on — the old Harvestide masks, worn since before anyone could say why, to keep the wandering spirits off — and the fiddles were going in the market square, and the first anyone in Triboar knew of the end was the wells. The wells went black. @[Black glass](the-glass) rose out of them like frozen water remembering how to climb, and out of the cellar-mouths, and out of the sluice-gates and the seams between cobbles, and it grew the way frost grows on a pane — in fans, in feathers, in reaching fingers — except that frost does not move against the wind, and does not choose.

It took people whole. That must be said exactly, because the party saw it in the way you see things in disasters, in shards that assemble later and wrongly: the glass did not cut, and did not crush. It closed. A dancer mid-turn, a fiddler mid-phrase, a mother reaching — taken standing, held like flies in amber, faces stilled mid-cry behind the black shine. The square filled with statues of the town. The party screamed themselves raw dragging people from its edges, and the edges kept arriving, and somewhere in the din the festival's bonfire went down under a wave of it with a hiss like the world being shushed.

And at the working's fringes, where feeling tore loose from the taken in rags, the night's last horror condensed: small wretched shapes of grief and fright, knitting themselves out of the shed emotion of a dying town — hunched things that wept and bit, pale grasping things that wanted only to be held and would drown you holding back. The party would not learn the name @[Sorrowsworn](the-sorrowsworn) for a long time. They learned everything else about them that night.

And the streets were no longer only glass and panic, because something was being born in them. Out of the night's grief — out of that much terror and loss compressed into one town on one evening — shapes were curdling in the dark between the lamps: half-formed things with too many reaching arms; a thing that wept as it came on, wept the whole time, and was not asking for comfort; a thing that screamed without ever drawing breath. They did not move like beasts. They moved like feelings that had been given bodies before their time. The party fought two of them at the Cross and a third at the mouth of the Everlund road — Iratius's fire made the weeping one recoil where steel had not, and Amyl found, to his lasting quiet horror, that one of them went still before him, briefly, as if in recognition — and no one had a name for any of it, and the night was not interested in providing one.

@[Edda Vell](edda-vell) died at the Tower door, and she died fighting — her sword left standing in one of the reaching things, her back to the wood, buying the last of the Tower's evacuees their thirty seconds. The Mistwarden of @[the Twelve](the-twelve), keeper of every key to a town that no longer had locks, holding the doorway against a surge of the wretched things while the refugee tail crowded through — and the party would remember her the way you remember the truly orderly in their last moments, because she died mid-task, key-ring in her fist, and the keys spilled down the steps after her like a chime. @[Mishka](mishka) was taken. The Northshield's landlady, who had promised a bearded fool he would scrub his own blood off her floor, went back into her own common room for the last of her lodgers and the glass came through the cellar she had once sent a firbolg down into, and it took her reaching — arm out, mouth open on someone's name. The party saw it through the window. There was nothing to be done, and they did not have time to learn how to live with that, because the night was not finished billing them.

@[Nik](nik) was taken at @[Sera's memorial](sera). He had fought the whole retreat beside them — rearguard, greataxe, grim as bedrock, the first friend this story ever gave them — and at the graveyard wall he stopped, and looked at the rising black where the yard's yews already stood entombed, and the party watching his face could see the arithmetic land: her memorial. Her new-raised memorial, that he had stood over that morning. He said — they would argue later about exactly what he said; the din ate half of it — something with twice in it. He would not leave her twice. And he went over the wall into the glass-light with his axe in his hand, a dwarf walking into the end of the world to stand over his wife's memorial, and the black closed the yard behind him like a book being shut, and that was Gurpnik: found her, lost her, and would not be moved. The party did not see him taken. They chose, forever after, to believe that mattered.

From the Tower stair, through the arrow-slits, the party saw the other salvation — the one nobody cast. West of the walls, where the dawn wagons had gone and the fleeing lines now streamed, the fields were full of running people and rising glass, and between the two, impossible and unhurried, walked the stag. Huge as a house. White as the far North. It moved along the refugee lines the way a sheepdog moves, wheeling the panicked toward the treeline, standing over the fallen until they rose — and where its hooves touched, the party could see it plainly even at that distance, the glass stopped. Fans of black reached the prints it left and broke around them like surf on pilings. The Harvest ran to the treeline of Gwaeron's Rest and found the forest already owned, and the god of trackers, who had blessed them once in hoofprints and asked their pardon for his absence, took the field at last on the night his wood's long corruption had been spent — shepherd to a running town, warden of the western dark. Hundreds lived because of it. The party watched between arrow-slits, climbing, and some of them wept going upstairs, which is the only direction it is worse to weep.

@[Darathra](darathra) held the portal room. She had been holding it — marshaling the stair, counting the rescued through the arch in tens, her blade in one hand and her voice in the square timbre it had boomed down from a balcony in gentler weather — and when the party came up last with Gregory asleep against Amyl's shoulder, she counted them too, by name, the way a Lord Protector counts. The glass was in the Tower by then. They could hear it coming up the stairwell, a sound like a frozen lake deciding. Go, she said. Count yourselves again on the other side. And then, while the arch took the last of the rescued, she did a strange, small, soldier's thing: pushed back her vambrace, uncapped the little ink-horn from her belt kit, and @[wrote something on her own forearm](the-testament) — quick, block letters, unhurried as a woman labeling a crate — and capped the horn, and rolled the vambrace down over it.

They argued with her. Of course they argued with her; you would have argued with her, and she would have already been three steps down. What she said, at the head of her burning stair, with her banners charring on the walls of the office she had held longer than some of the party had been alive, was: A Lord Protector goes where her town goes. And she went down. Down her own stair, into the rising black, upright, unhurried, one hand on the rail — and the glass came up to meet her and took her mid-step, like a woman going down to answer her own door. The last the party saw of Darathra Shendrel, before the arch's silver closed over them, was the crown of her iron-gray head inside the black shine, descending. She had looked at the rising glass, Tirig said later — and Tirig had grown up reading weather and dooms — the way one studies a road. Not a grave. Nobody knew what to do with that, so they grieved, which is what you do with everything, in the end.

And on a low rise west of the falling town, out past the fields where the stag worked and the glass could not go, a figure stood watching. Small — slight as a clerk — plainly dressed for the cold, hands folded, alone. She did not gesture, and nothing attended her, and no line of the working ran to her that any eye could see. She only watched the black rise through Triboar with the stillness of an auditor confirming a column of figures — and when the last light went out of the market square, she inclined her head to the town. A small bow. Courtesy, or acknowledgment, or something that has no name because so few people have ever needed one for it. Then she turned and walked north, unhurried, and the night took her.

The company stood that first night in a hall of quiet marble — seven of them, and a sleeping boy, and a future in more pieces than anyone could carry in one direction — and someone, unbidden, began to hum. @[Aelendir](aelendir), they would all swear later, though Aelendir swore his lips had been still. @[The tune](the-tune) moved through the strange hall the way it had moved through a taproom a season and a lifetime ago, and every one of them let it finish. The counting, the choosing, the dividing of roads — all of that belonged to the days ahead. But @[the doors](the-doors) — though none of them would have called them that yet — had already begun, one by one, to open.
