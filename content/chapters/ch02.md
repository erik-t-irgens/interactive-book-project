# II. Bloodrot
*In which a good deed is repaid, and the forest is found to be bleeding*

Gratitude, when it comes wrapped in fear, is a strange thing to receive.

Kirsten's mother found the party before the dust of Ransor's Open Road was out of their clothes. The children had run home ahead of them; now she came with Kirsten carried in her arms and @[Gregory](gregory) pressed close at her side, and thanked them for both her children's lives with a warmth that never quite reached her eyes. She was watchful in the way of someone with a great deal to lose, and her gratitude, though real, kept glancing over its own shoulder. She pressed on them a key her daughter had found — to a chest hidden in the upper reaches of Ransor's Open Road, overlooked in the urgency of the rescue — and told them what was inside was theirs, for what they had done. They went and opened it. A pouch that gave forth any spice a cook could name; a cloak that took on the seeming of any garment; a pendant that let the wearer, thrice a day, hear the surface of another's thoughts. Not the tools of a poor family. Not the sort of thing one leaves behind by accident.

*She said more than she meant to, and less than they wanted. She had come to Triboar for a reason, she allowed — not driven here like the others, not fleeing anything, but arrived, on some purpose of her own that bore on her children and the future she wanted for them. She would not say what. And the party noted, as they had begun to note everything, a small strangeness that sat oddly in the mind: in all of Triboar, swollen as it was with the displaced and the desperate, there were no other tieflings. None but this family, and @[Iratius](iratius). Whatever had emptied the roads of every other soul with infernal blood in their veins, it had not touched these two households — and no one could say why.*

@[Amyl](amyl), for his part, carried the pit's unease out of it with him. The @[presence beneath the town](the-quiet-thing) had not gone silent so much as gone quiet, the way a held breath is quiet; the frantic terror of the rat-swarm had settled into a low and constant dread that followed him from the ruin to the market to the road, faint but never absent. @[Corvyn](corvyn) felt it too, the both of them tuned now to a frequency the others could not hear. It was no longer speaking. But it was still there, and it was still afraid, and it had not forgotten them any more than they could forget it.

---

Their business, that day, was a missing woman. @[Sera](sera) — one of @[the Twelve](the-twelve) who kept the town — had not returned from the western wood, and the party set out to find her along the tree-line of Gwaeron's Rest. They did not go alone. @[Nik](nik) came with them, the stern dwarf who had carried @[Tirig](tirig) in from the cold; and it became plain, as they walked, that this was no idle duty for him. He would not say much. But he flinched at the depth of the forest, and would not go past the patrol-paths without cause, and there was a tightness in him that had nothing to do with the cold. The party did not yet understand the whole of it. They understood only that a hard man was frightened, and that his fear had a name he was not speaking.

*It was here, in the going, that Tirig showed them the first of what she was. The trail grew faint and then vanished, and where the others saw only leaf-litter and shadow she saw a story written in bent grass and pressed earth — and to read it better she changed, her shape flowing into something keener-nosed and low to the ground, and led them on. A shapeshifter. None of them had known. It was the first of many quiet unveilings among people who had woken knowing one another's hearts but none of their histories.*

And then the forest began to bleed. It was on the plants first — a black ichor beading from leaf and stem like sap gone wrong, dark and glistening. Tirig touched it, and tasted it, and found it cold: not the coolness of shade but a cold that did not lift, that sat in the palm and refused the warmth of living skin and would not be warmed however long it was held. It was sweet. It grew thicker as they pressed deeper, sparser as they looked back toward the town, as though something at the heart of the wood were the source of it and the corruption thinned with distance. The wildlife wore it. And nothing about it was right.

---

They found the letters strewn across the forest floor before they found what had scattered them. Love letters, each numbered in its corner by the same careful hand — seven of them present, of a sequence the numbering said ran to eight — and each signed with a single initial — D — in a script that labored over its own tenderness. It was Tirig who read the ground around them and gave the scattered pages their story: a satchel torn from a shoulder, its strap ripped through; churned earth that spoke of a struggle; a trail leading deeper in. She tracked it step by patient step, and Nik followed at her back with a dread that thickened at every sign — the letters, the torn satchel, the violence written in the mud — because it was his wife who was missing in this wood, and every one of these things belonged to the shape of an answer he did not want. The trail ended at a camp: ransacked, a tent half-collapsed, the signs of something that had gone very wrong in this place.

Inside the tent was a hand. A woman's hand, severed clean, dainty and pale — and closed within its curled fingers, a scrap of torn parchment. The party worked it loose. Four words survived the tear, in a firm and unhurried script: @[Prepare for Harvest](the-harvest). —Verity.

They found Sera at the edge of the camp, beneath a bush whose low branches had half-hidden her — from the weather, or from any eye not searching, or simply because that was where she had fallen and the wood had already begun, indifferently, to take her in. There is no kind way to find the dead. She had been a handsome woman in the middle of her life, dark hair threaded with silver-gray at the temples, her amber eyes closed now, the plain sun-stitched shawl she was known for still pinned at her shoulder against a cold she no longer felt. She had been one of the Twelve, a protector of this town, a woman whose laughter someone in that small search party could have described from memory — and she lay under a hedge in the cold, with the letters of her secret heart scattered through the mud behind her, and the wood going on around her as though nothing at all had been subtracted from the world. She had not merely been killed; she had been killed with intention, in a manner that @[Sov](sov) — who knew the shapes such things took — recognized at once as ritual. Something had been done here, some working for which her death was the instrument. Clutched in her hand, torn from a throat, was a pendant: broken, bloodied, and bearing on its face a symbol Sov knew too well. The mark of @[Shar](shar), the Lady of Loss, goddess of the dark behind the stars. And on its reverse, etched by a patient hand into the metal, a second sign that belonged to no faith he could name — a straight line, and a downward chevron breaking through it, as if something were being driven across a border, or a veil were being pierced from above.

*A last letter was folded in her grip, and this one was hers: her reply, the eighth of the set, the one that completed the correspondence and confirmed what the others implied. Sera had loved this D, whoever he was, and had written back to him, and had carried his words and her own into the wood on the night she died.*

---

They carried her home as the light failed. Triboar met them with a pyre already rising in the square and a grief that had nowhere to settle, and when they brought the letters to light, the town's suspicion turned in every direction at once. Who was D? None could say. No one was above the question — not Darathra, not @[Nik](nik), not the party themselves. And it was Nik who broke, in the end; Nik who read the letters through after the first shock had passed, and understood something in them that the others could only watch land on him. His grief, whatever its exact shape, was real past any doubting. The party filed it away with everything else they could not yet explain: a hard man, undone, holding the evidence of a love that had not been his.

They brought their findings to the Tower — and they brought their suspicions with them, for by now no one in Triboar sat entirely outside the question, the Lord Protector included. @[Darathra](darathra) did not invite them in. She came out to them instead, and stood on the stoop of her own door to hear it all, framed against the banners — and it was the first time any of them had seen her close. The voice that had boomed down from the balcony belonged to a woman worn like good leather: iron-gray hair pulled back with a soldier's economy, a face weathered by more seasons of frontier office than any election had promised her, and pale, watchful eyes that did the listening her mouth was too disciplined to do. She wore her authority the way she wore the plain, hard-used gear beneath the mantle of office — as a working tool, not an ornament. She heard it all, and knew the name Verity no better than they did — @[Corvyn](corvyn), listening past her words to the thoughts beneath, found no recognition there, only the same sincere and baffled grief the whole town wore. But there was one thing she was not saying. Beneath the surface of her mind, plain to the pendant's borrowed sight, sat an orc and a locked cell, insistent and unwelcome, circled round with a wariness she let no one past. When the party asked her about the orc — the one the mob had named, the one she had denied — her face closed like a door. She took Sera's body gently from them, and thanked them, and stepped back inside, and the door swung shut and the bolt slid home behind her.

Sleep took them, and the gray took them, as it had before. The same colorless expanse, the same circle of silhouettes that were themselves and each other. But this time they were not alone in it. Far off, at the edge of what could be seen, a sixth figure stood — gaunt, and turned away, and too distant to make out. It did not approach. It did not speak. It only stood at the threshold of the dream, one more shape in the gray, and the party woke without knowing whether it had been watching them, or waiting, or simply lost in the same fog they were.
