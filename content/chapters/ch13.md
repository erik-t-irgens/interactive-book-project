# XIII. The Heartsilver Evening
*In which a promise is collected, a room is read, and applause dies badly*

@audio: heartsilver-eve fade=6
The engagement itself could not decently be postponed a second time — a promise made over cards survives most things, and Lady Mariel had made certain it survived Triboar. Her house stood in the garden district with its windows already gold. The Lady herself proved to be a portly, genuinely beautiful woman upholstered in silks chosen by someone with perfect taste and paid to exercise it — an empress of the salons whose empire was entirely made of other people's regard, and who governed it full-time. She received @[Aelendir](aelendir) the way collectors receive a masterpiece returned from loan, and @[Corvyn](corvyn), by extension, as a fortunate acquisition of the same lot — her affection for them both perfectly real and perfectly proprietary, a patroness's love, which is a love with a ledger in it. Within the first quarter hour they watched her send back a faultless wine, complain warmly to the waitstaff about nothing, and be adored for it, and the party began to understand the terms: her hall held perhaps ninety guests, which in Silverymoon means everyone, and every one of them was, in some fraction, hers.

They had learned by then that the legation would attend — legations attend everything — and in the cloakroom before the performance the party held a council of war lasting ninety seconds. @[Corvyn](corvyn), who would be otherwise engaged on stage, delegated with a performer's economy: “I'll be lulling the room. You lot read it. Gracefully — nobody corners a diplomat at a party. You orbit her, you let her shine at you, and you count what she chooses to shine about.”

Corvyn and Aelendir played. It does the record no good to attempt the set list; what matters is that the gnome and the eladrin had performed together exactly once before, at a masquerade that ended in arrests, and that whatever had begun between their instruments that night now finished becoming itself. The hall forgot to eat. A Knight in Silver was observed to weep with excellent posture. And there was one melody they did not play. They had not discussed not playing it. Some agreements get made below the level of speech, in the place where both of them kept the @[tune](the-tune) the whole party knew and none of them could source — and neither man so much as let his fingers drift toward it, all evening, the way you do not say a name in a room where you suspect the name can hear.

In the interval the party worked the room, because a party is also an instrument. Boar's Rest had been a masquerade in the full sense — names given truly, faces never — so what they recognized among Lady Mariel's guests were not faces but names wearing faces for the first time: introductions they had banked a season ago now paying out in flesh. A mining factor from Mirabar whose laugh they knew a full second before her name confirmed it. A grain magnate who had talked tariffs at Hyuth's buffet and was visibly startled to find the evening's musicians attached to the survivors of the town he'd been tariffing. And from the factor, over excellent brandy, a thread worth keeping: talk of a traveling purveyor of appetites and assurances, working the roads south and east under a newer name since autumn, always a town ahead of wherever he was asked after. @[Iratius](iratius) listened with a stillness that had nothing polite in it. At his hip, wrapped and peace-bonded per the invitation's etiquette, the sword — the sword he had taken, privately, to calling @[Volcaris](volcaris), because the mirror had given him the word and the blade had answered it with fire, and a thing that answers to a word deserves to wear it — warmed. Briefly. Absurdly. In a ballroom, toward no bearing at all. And then it subsided, like a hound that has scented something on a wind and lost it.

And one reunion required no masks, no names, and no mercy: threading the crowd toward them with double-barreled delight came Irska and Fenn — of Silverymoon, it turned out, as such creatures inevitably are, wintering among the salons whenever the roads stopped paying. They had seen the party's faces bare at the fittings; they recognized them instantly, embraced Corvyn like revenue, and expressed their sorrow at Triboar's fate for the length of exactly one completed sentence. “A tragedy—” “—an unpaid one.” For the fall of a town figured in the twins' cosmology chiefly as a billing obstruction: the finest commissions of their season entombed in glass, and the tab — the Lord Protector's own tab, personally guaranteed — gone to the grave, or wherever it was towns went now. “We do not blame the dear Protector—” “—we invoice her estate.” They said it with perfect poise and unbreakable smiles, and the party's sympathy died somewhere between the first sentence and its completion; and @[Tirig](tirig), who had personally signed that tab wearing a dead woman's face, bought them one drink apiece and said nothing at all.

@[Clarity](clarity) found them at the second interval. Up close the legate resolved into a tiefling woman of middle years with sommelier's poise and a diplomat's miraculous attention, the kind that makes each person addressed briefly the only person in the North. Her compliments on the performance were precise as surgery — she had noticed what was hardest in it, and said so, correctly. She spoke of her young city with the uncomplicated fondness of a gardener: streets going in, a harbor of sorts, room — this with a smile at the crowded hall — for everyone the old world had misplaced. She never once said anything false. That was the horror of her, though it took the party days to name it: she was exactly what she appeared to be, a sincere woman doing sincere work for a machine she believed was mercy. @[Tirig](tirig) asked her one question — lightly, in passing, the way you test ice: what did @[Fundament](fundament) want with Silverymoon? And the legate's answer came warm and instant and perfectly shaped: “Friends before winter. The same thing everyone wants.” It was gracious, and it was true, and it was nothing at all, and it fit in the hand like a stone polished by ten thousand identical askings. When she moved on, trailing warmth, @[Saeron](saeron) looked at his own hands for a while. He had felt the room's affection for her, and it had been genuine, and so had hers for the room, and none of it helped.

The set ended. The applause rose — and the doors opened on it, both leaves, unhurried, and the applause died in sections, from the back of the hall forward, the way a field goes quiet under a moving cloud. Five figures came up the center aisle. Gray robes. Bare feet on the Lady's marble. Lines of white ash drawn across their brows — and @[Amyl](amyl), who had been standing by the musicians with a cup he'd forgotten to drink from for an hour, set it down carefully, as if the cup were the thing that needed protecting.

@[The eldest warden](the-eldest-warden)'s voice was not raised and did not need to be; it was a voice trained under trees. @[Amyl Rachis](amyl). Novice under watch. You are called to answer before the assembled circles: for workings of the Blighted path, and for complicity in the blight at Triboar, whose wound is in every grove from here to the ice. The hall's silence had a texture to it now, ninety people learning simultaneously that the evening had become a story they would be telling for years. @[Tirig](tirig) had moved before anyone saw her decide to; @[Iratius](iratius)'s hand was somewhere it shouldn't have been, given the peace-bonds; and it was Amyl who stopped it all — one enormous hand, raised flat, patient as a door. He apologized. Of course he apologized. To the wardens, for the venue. To the Lady, for her marble. To the room — I am sorry; the music was so lovely, and now this will be what everyone remembers of the evening — and to his friends last of all, quietly, in the voice of a man who had been packing for this journey his whole life without admitting he owned the luggage: “I will go. It is my circle. If I am asked, I would like it very much if you came west for the answering.”

Circle law is old and unbothered by lords. The eldest warden, not unkindly, laid it out for the Lady's protesting steward as one explains weather: the accused would be held at the grove-hall outside the walls; the rite-day fell seven days hence, when the circles' seniors could stand assembled beneath the boughs of the @[Grandfather Tree](the-grandfather-tree); he would be moved then, and tried there, under leaf and law, and attendance was the circles' to grant — which, said the warden, with a glance at the party that had done its research, it would be. Nine days. The wardens went out with their prisoner walking free-handed in their midst, gray through the gold doorway, and the last thing the hall heard was Amyl's voice, apologizing to the night porter about the lateness of the hour.

Lady Mariel stood in her emptying hall like a general surveying weather. “My house does not forget its guests,” she said, to the party generally and the world at large. “Nine days is time enough to be useful in, and I intend to spend it that way, and so, I imagine, do you.” Outside, the garden district was dark and sweet. Above the roofs, over the river, the Moonbridge glowed its four-century glow — and flickered, once, as they watched, like something agreeing with something.

It emerged that being useful included being housed. The Heartsilver guest wing was opened that same night — “My musicians do not lodge at inns,” the Lady said, in the tone of a woman closing a ledger — and for the days that followed her estate in the garden district became the company's fixed point: beds, board, a rehearsal room nobody dared decline, and staff whose watchfulness was included in the patronage. The cost of all this hospitality was entered invisibly, no one doubted, in the great account of what Lady Mariel Heartsilver was owed by the world; but the walls were good, and the table was better, and a company being circled by watchers could do worse than a fortress run by a woman who audited her own wine.
