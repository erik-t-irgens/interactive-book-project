# XXXIX. Happy, Not Truly
*In which a supper is kept, a folder is opened, and a boy arrives at the gates*

@audio: fundament-doubled fade=6
@[Darathra](darathra)'s ward lies inland of the harbor, up where the elder streets widen and the intake dormitories stand in ranks of honest new timber, and the party walked up into it at dusk the next day and read her hand on everything they passed: cisterns cleaned and chalk-dated; a duty roster nailed under an awning where rain could not get it; the eastern dormitory roofed at last, its new beams still bleeding sap, its lamplit doorway full of children being counted in to supper by a warden who was enjoying it. A town is a tool that takes the shape of the hand that holds it, and this ward, whatever flag it flew, had been picked up by the same hand that once held Triboar; and the party climbed through it in silence, and were glad, and grieved, in the exact proportion this city always mixes.

@audio: none fade=8
The captain's house is small, spare, and set square to its street like a helm on a shelf: one room down, one up, a soldier's garden of onions and remedies, a door that has been oiled against squeaking out of pure principle. She met them at it with her sleeves rolled and no vambraces — the first time in two towns and a year that any of them had seen her forearms bare — and the skin of the left one was clean, unmarked, scrubbed; and every one of the seven marked it, and marked each other marking it, and said nothing yet. She had cooked. Plainly, hugely, herself: a stew with a whole garden in it, bread with a bottom crust you could sole boots with, and she stood over them while they filled plates with the fierce ceremonial hospitality of a woman who has fed musters from a wagon-gate, and only when every plate was loaded did she sit, and square her spoon to her bowl like a paper to a table's edge, and say: “Runners are dismissed. The door is oiled. Everything. Start where the fire started.”

They gave her the year. It took the stew, the second helping, and most of the bread, and this book has carried every chapter of it and will not carry them twice; what it will carry is how she listened. She listened the way she musters: in order, completely, holding her questions in file until each of them earned its turn, and the questions, when they came, were a commander's — numbers, dates, distances; who held the door and who counted the dead; what the orc carved on the post and which word faced outward on the stair. She did not once say impossible. She heard the doors and the visions; the North and the socket in the mountain wall; the Feywild's pocketed days; Nesmé standing and the glass breaking; the crossing, the gray country, the dead on their roads; the fortress that was a horizon and the man they carried out of it twice — once from an aisle of glass, once from a doorway in the Hells — and at each of the places where a listener may choose between doubt and attention, she chose attention, leaning forward slightly, the way she leaned over maps. Only once did she stop them. It was at the wool-house, at the shard, at the word the party made themselves say at a fire outside Nesmé; and she looked at @[Sov](sov) for a long moment, at the man eating her stew with a seam of pale glass under his shirt, and said, “And you walked into the country of the dead and fetched him back out by asking nicely,” and Sov said, “They asked nicely,” and @[Darathra Shendrel](darathra) put down her spoon, and pressed the heel of her hand once, briefly, against her mouth, and picked the spoon back up, and said, “Go on.”

When the year was spent she sat with it a while, the way she sits with casualty lists — respectfully, without flinching, doing the arithmetic — and then she stood, and went to the campaign chest under the stair, and came back with a folder. Oilcloth, tied, worn at the corners from handling by exactly one pair of hands. “My turn,” she said. “I have been a skeptic in a city of the grateful for one season, with no proof, no ally, and one piece of paper in my own handwriting that I do not remember writing. You will tell me what it means, because I believe you are the only seven people alive who can.” And she untied it, and laid it out. Intake manifests, copied small and dense: names that arrive with no origins; origins that arrive with no names; a column she had reconciled four times that would not reconcile. A page of dates in her own shorthand — the advance lists, the ones that come down to the wards before their intakes exist, which she had begun, quietly, to collect. And on top of all of it, alone, a single sheet in careful block capitals: @[a transcription](the-testament), she said, of what she had found on her own left forearm on her first morning in this city, under a vambrace she did not remember strapping on, in her own unmistakable field hand — and had copied out twice, and washed off, because a ward-captain cannot be seen wearing madness on her arm; and had carried since, folded, like a wound she could not locate. “I know what it is,” she said, before any of them could ask. “That is the trouble. The first time death missed me — I was twenty-two, a green lieutenant with a shield-wall gone and no right to be breathing — I made myself a promise, and I have kept it my whole service since, the way you keep a weapon you pray never to draw: that when my death came certain — certain, not likely — I would not meet it scrambling. I would stop. I would write myself down, plainly, by my own hand, and go in order. A rite. Mine. Private. No one living has ever heard of it, and I have never once begun that letter, in forty years of close things, because it is for one hour of a life only, and the hour had never come.” She laid two fingers on the sheet. “This is that letter. Finished. In my hand. It was on my arm in a city where they tell me I was saved in time. So either the page lies — and it is my hand — or I wrote it with my death standing in the doorway, and the doorway was real, and everything this city has told me about that night is a lie built exactly the size of my death.” She turned the sheet to face them. “Tell me,” said the @[Lord Protector of Triboar](darathra), “when I wrote this.”

They did not have to read it. That is the moment the whole supper had been walking toward, and it arrived the way the large moments of this book have learned to arrive, quietly, over bread: @[Tirig](tirig) looked at the block capitals for the length of one breath, and then looked up at @[Darathra](darathra), and recited the page from memory before her eyes ever reached it — all of it, word for word, in the careful level voice she keeps for things that must not be dropped: I am Darathra Shendrel, Lord Protector of Triboar. The children are through. The muster is honest. The town goes together, and I go with it, by my own choice, knowing. No rescue. No regret. Tell nobody I was brave; I was ready. Written by my own hand, at my death, as promised. — and around the table the others came in like a rite, @[Amyl](amyl) and @[Saeron](saeron) and @[Aelendir](aelendir) each carrying a sentence of it, because all seven of them had stood in the Lord Protector's tower under a dying sky and watched that arm be written. “The children went through first,” Tirig said. “Forty. We counted them through; you showed them the way up the tower with a lamp in each hand. And at the head of the arch, while it took the last of them, you pushed back the vambrace and uncapped the ink-horn from your belt kit and wrote this — standing, unhurried, like a woman labeling a crate — and rolled the vambrace down over it, and went back down your own stairs. You had guessed — alone, with no proof, exactly like now — that whoever the glass took would not be allowed to keep the taking. You wrote yourself a message through the forgetting, Darathra. And at the bottom of the tower you gave Amyl the cobbler's name, and you said” — and here Tirig's voice, which does not break, bent — “you said, A Lord Protector goes where her town goes. And you walked down into the glass like a woman going down to answer her own door.”

The silence that followed had furniture in it: the lamp, the folder, the clean scrubbed forearm lying on the table like evidence of a theft. Darathra sat through it at attention. And when she finally spoke, she asked none of the questions the party had braced for — not how, not why her, not what the glass was — she asked, in the voice she keeps for musters, very quietly: “Was I afraid?” And @[Tirig](tirig), who had been waiting a season and two worlds to give this report to its rightful officer, said: “You looked at it the way one studies a road. Not a grave.” And @[Darathra Shendrel](darathra), Lord Protector of a town that stands nowhere and everywhere, took that in the way she takes all reports, with a single nod — and then she stood up, and walked to the window, and stood with her back to the table and her hands finding each other behind it, parade rest, while her shoulders did, twice, a thing the party will not describe further because some things even guests do not watch; and then she came back, and sat down, and squared the transcription to the table's edge. “Understand what you have just done,” she said. “A season ago I woke on a quay with a story in my ears and a hole in my night, and I have stood in that hole every morning since, wondering which of us was mad — me, or a city of the kind. You have handed me the worst gift a soldier can be given and the only one I wanted. I was at my best, and it was taken from me. I chose, and the choosing was taken. They did not just steal my town.” She touched the clean forearm, once. “They stole my consent to follow it. That is the crime, children. Write it exactly that way in whatever you are keeping. Not the taking. The forgetting.” And then, after a moment, quietly, in the voice of a woman closing a muster: “I kept my promise. I intend to remember keeping it.”

What remained of the evening was a council of war disguised as the washing-up. She would pull the advance lists — all of them, every ward, through channels that owed her nails. She would map the pending columns; she named the manifests' strange category herself, flatly, the way she names things: “There are people in my paperwork who are logged as @[in transit](the-in-transit-column) and have been in transit for years. Nobody transits for years. Either the column is a lie, or there is a place between the shores, and it has a waiting room.” — and @[Saeron](saeron) went so still at the table's end that @[Bettany](bettany-and-osric)'s iron key could be heard to shift against his chest when he finally breathed. And she set the discipline, count on your fingers, all of you: @[Nik](nik) was not to be told — not yet — and she gave the reason like a field order and it held the whole cruelty of this city in four sentences: “Nik knows the night was wrong. He knew it before any of us; he was standing at her grave when the wrongness came. The doctrine gave him somewhere to put it, and the somewhere holds. Do not take a man's shelf away until you can hand him what goes in its place.”

They went to the wall the next morning anyway — not to tell him; to help. He was there, of course, cloth in hand, moving down the columns of the lost of Triboar, polishing each name as he came to it, unhurried, methodical, the way he stacked wood in another life; and he stood very still when he saw them, and counted them twice with his eyes, and nodded once, as though a long dispute inside him had finally been ruled on, and said, “Good.” They took corners of the cloth and worked the wall with him an hour, seven names at a time, and he told them, unasked, in a voice with nine kinds of weather in it: “They put her name up. I told them she died before — in the before; that it wasn't the same — they put it up anyway. Every name they could gather. I keep it clean. It is all wrong, and it is a good wall.” And at the end, as they were leaving, he stopped @[Saeron](saeron) with two fingers on his sleeve — only Saeron — and said, quietly, with his eyes on the wall: “You were there after. In the graveyard. I remember more than I say at the shrine.” And went back to his names, and left the sentence standing in the morning air behind them like a door neither locked nor open.

The in-transit column broke open two days later, at a records desk, with @[Darathra](darathra) standing over the clerk like weather. The pending names ran back years — back before Triboar, before the North Ride, back to intakes from towns the party had never heard the doctrine mention — and among the oldest, in a ledger gone soft at the spine, under a heading from a city on the Chionthar: Iriaebor. @[Saeron](saeron) read the column standing, without a sound, one hand flat on the desk and the other at his chest, at the key; and the column read like the roll of a fire he had set half a life ago — the barracks' detained, the cull's gathered, the ones the breaking mirror lost between — names he had carried as ash for years, logged here in a clerk's patient hand as neither dead nor arrived; and third from the bottom, @[in transit these nine years](the-in-transit-column), recoverable pending capacity: a mason's name. @[Bettany's husband](bettany-and-osric). Saeron stood over it a long time. Then he copied the whole column twice — once for the folder, once for the breast pocket where the key and the cords live — and he said, to Darathra, in the flat voice he keeps for vows because the other voices shake: “Recoverable. Their own ledger says recoverable. Whatever else we do in this city, we are emptying that column.” And Darathra initialed the requisition she had already written, and said, “That is the correct use of paperwork,” and the two of them have understood each other completely from that hour to this.

@[The Oracle](the-oracle) came for @[Sov](sov) at dusk that same day, on foot, alone, in the gray robes of his office, and stood at the foot of the lodging stair until Sov came down; and they walked the eastern rampart together in the failing doubled light, the man with glass in his chest and the man with nothing in his name, and what passed between them is set down here as Sov chose to set it down, plainly, with the terms left in. He was told his mother's name, which he knew — Lenore, of Ormath — and the shape of her ending, which he did not: the sickness, the crossing, the meeting on a gray road with an officer of a country she was not done with; and the bargain — her return, bought at the standard rate, which is service; an agent of a design that was not hers and was not his; a son. “You were the consideration clause,” the Oracle said, in the voice of a man who has read the instrument many times. “She understood that. She signed anyway. She wanted the rest of her life, and she wanted it to contain you, and it did, and it does. Your mother kept her side. I have tried to keep mine.” And Sov, who had stood before a pane in a fortress and studied a bargaining woman's lamplit face without knowing why it fit him, understood at last what the Vault had shown him and declined to caption: the small warm room; the palm going flat on the table; the gray-eyed visitor at the edge of the light. He had already seen his parents meet. The collection keeps everything, and shows you what you cannot yet spend.

He asked about the seam before he asked anything else, because he is @[Sov](sov), and the ledger of his body comes before the ledger of his heart. The Oracle looked at it a while — at the shirt over it — with the professional regard of a man assessing a colleague's stitching. “It is held,” he said, “which you know. Held is a term. Nothing she holds is spent — that is the mercy of her, and the whole of it. And nothing she holds is free — that is the rest of her. The glass in you is a note payable, Sov Thimron, and she does not call her notes early. She @[waits to be asked](the-raven-queen). Everything in her country waits to be asked; it is the only law she has ever kept.” And then, because the light was going and the office never quite leaves the man: “When the asking comes — and it is coming; I have spoken for it in session — read the terms yourself. All of them. Twice. You are the son of the two best readers of terms I ever met, and one of them out-negotiated death itself with nothing in her hand but the rest of a life.” It was as close to tenderness as the office allows. Sov took it the way he takes everything now, steadily, into the held place; and asked, at the rampart's end, the only question left — “Did you love her?” — and the @[Oracle of Truth](the-oracle) stood with both hands on the old stone, looking out over the fade going dark, and gave his son the expensive kind of nothing for a long time, and then said: “I have kept her bargain for thirty years after the party of the second part was dead. Read the terms yourself.”

@audio: fundament-doubled fade=6
That night the seven sat late on the lodging stairs, as has become their custom, fitting the days together — the folder, the column, the wall, the rampart — and the fit was the chapter's title before this book knew it needed one. The city below them glowed and worked and sang somewhere, badly, happily; and the happiness was real, and the city was a theft; and the taken were home, and home was the crime; and a woman who chose her taking wept at parade rest for the choosing she was not allowed to keep; and a man polished a wrong name on a good wall and remembered more than he said; and a column of the neither-dead-nor-arrived waited in a ledger for capacity; and every one of these things was true at once, all the way down, and the party sat with the sum of it in the dark. It was @[Amyl](amyl), in the end, who found the words this chapter is named for, and he said them to the doubled sky in the voice he uses for reading sickness with his hands, gently, without appeal: “They are happy. Not truly. Not falsely, either — that is what I cannot forgive it. Happy, not truly — and nobody in this city gets to choose which, and that is the whole of the wrong, and it is the same wrong from the ferry to the throne of it.”

The advance lists came up from the records desk on the third day — every ward, a season deep — and @[Darathra](darathra) spread them on the captain's table like a campaign map, and they read clean: dates, counts, dormitory assignments, the grim tidy grammar of a city that plans its mercies. Except for one page. One list had no date. Names — dozens of them, a town's worth of trades and households, smith and miller and ferry-keep — entered in the @[square devout hand](verity), complete, confirmed, and undated: a harvest scheduled the way you schedule weather, certain of everything but the hour. There was no origin column. There never is. The party sat over that page late, all seven and a Lord Protector, doing what could be done, which was nothing: no town named, no road to ride, no bell they could reach to ring. And Darathra stood at her window at parade rest, looking east over her lamplit ward, and said the thing this chapter keeps: “Somewhere out there is a town that is already in my paperwork. They are eating supper tonight. They are mending for winter. And I cannot so much as tell them to look at the sky.” The days were beginning to close — all seven of them could feel it now, the way you feel weather before the glass falls — and the party went to their beds knowing the shape of the next thing and not its name; and far out in the dark over the eastern desert, past anyone's lamplight, the world went on being quiet, in the way the world is quiet before bells.
