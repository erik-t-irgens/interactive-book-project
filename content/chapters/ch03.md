# III. Time & Tide
*In which a girl mourns in a country of impossible color, and a stranger grieves behind a closed door*

@audio: Time-&-Tide fade=4 once
The gray did not hold them this time. It changed.

They had gathered again in that colorless place, the circle of themselves and the sixth shape standing off at the edge of it. And this time they went to it. They crossed that impossible distance toward the gaunt figure turned away in the fog — and as they neared, it turned to meet them, and for a moment its face was almost there, almost a face, before it thinned and faded and was gone. But where it had stood the world did not stay empty. Color came pouring in to fill it.

*Such color. More than Faerûn had ever shown any of them.* A @[walkway of sun-bleached stone](the-bright-country) unrolled beneath their feet, threading across shallow clear water that caught the light and threw it back in hues too bright to be real. Trees stood in the distance in colors no forest wears. The footbridges converged, far off, beneath a single great tree that hid some larger structure behind its boughs, and two tall statues flanked it, weathered by an age the party could only guess at. At the tree's root sat a shape of worked stone, deliberate, placed. It was beautiful the way a held note is beautiful, and it did not feel like a place so much as a memory of a place — though whose, and how they had come to be standing inside it, none of them could have said. Nor could they say what it was they stood within at all: a memory, a vision of something yet to come, another realm laid alongside their own, or a thing happening even now in some place their bodies had never been. The dream declined to say.

---

They went toward the great tree slowly, wary as trespassers in all that impossible color — and it was only as they drew near that they saw what the roots enclosed. A grave. Worked stone, deliberate and old, set where the tree could hold it. And at the grave, @[a girl](the-girl-at-the-grave).

She was small, and she was grieving, and around her stood figures who spoke to one another in words the party could not hear — mourners at some rite, their voices swallowed before they carried. The impression of the scene came through muffled and obscured, as though the party watched it from underwater, or through the wrong end of a great many years. They tried to reach her. Words went out and died in the air between; hands, gently offered, passed through her shoulder as through smoke, and she did not seem to notice them at all — whatever she was, she was not there for them, or they were not there for her. They could feel the shape of the grief without being told its cause. A true death. Something that would not come back. And when the others had drifted away and left the girl alone at the stone, she knelt, and she worked a spell over it — a reaching-out, a calling, the party knew it for what it was: an attempt to speak with the dead. To ask a question of someone already gone. To not let go.

It failed. Or worse than failed — it answered wrongly. The reaching-out called something, but not the beloved dead; shadows tore loose from the bright air and came for the living, and the dream turned all at once from grief to violence. The party fought. It was hard, and it was ugly, and @[Tirig](tirig) fell more than once beneath the clawing dark and was hauled back each time from the edge of it. They fought as the girl's small figure looked on, or did not — it was hard to say what she saw. And when the last shadow was undone, she was gone, and the grave stood alone.

They went to read the name on the stone. *They could not.* Not because the letters were worn, or written in some script they did not know — the marks were plain and whole and there. But something would not let the eye hold them. The name slid out of perception the instant it was grasped, refused the mind the way two like poles of a lodestone refuse each other, present and unreadable at once. Someone, or something, had seen to it that this name could not be known. And on that impossibility the dream released them, and they woke — and Tirig, who had gone down more than once beneath the clawing shadows, woke whole: not a mark on her, none of the dream's wounds carried back across into the waking world.

They woke to sunrise, and to smoke, and to the whole of Triboar gathered in the square to send @[Sera](sera) on. The pyre stood built and waiting. @[Nik](nik) was nowhere among the crowd. And Darathra climbed to where the town could see her, and spoke.

— set down as near as this record can carry it —

@[Darathra](darathra) did not climb the dais so much as take it, the way she took everything: squarely, as a duty. She stood a moment with the paper in her hand, and then she folded the paper and put it away, and the square went quiet enough to hear the lanterns burn.

*“I wrote a speech,”* she said. *“It was a good speech. It said Sera was a light in dark times, and a protector of this town, and all of it is true and none of it is her.”* Somewhere near the front a child asked a question and was gently hushed; Darathra waited for the hush, and thanked the child with a look. *“Here is her. Sera knew your names. All of them — the new ones off the wagons too, the ones the rest of us are still learning. She walked the north gate in the cold so that men twenty years her senior would not have to, and she never once let them learn she had arranged it. She lost at cards on purpose to people who needed the coin and the dignity both, and she was terrible at losing convincingly, and everyone let her believe otherwise, because the lie was the kindest thing anyone could afford to give her back.”*

The wind moved through the square and took a few lanterns' flames sideways, and nobody moved to shield them. *“One of the Twelve is dead,”* Darathra said, and here, for two words, the Lord Protector's voice cracked and was permitted to. *“My friend is dead. I do not know yet what took her from us, and I will not lie to you from this stage and call it settled. I will find it. That is my word, and you have never known me to spend my word lightly. Tonight we do not mourn what Triboar has lost. Tonight we are grateful — grieving, and grateful — for what we had, longer than we deserved, brighter than we noticed. Her name was Sera. Say it kindly, and say it often, and she is not entirely gone.”*

*“As the Harvest Festival draws near, we are reminded of the cycle of life and death that touches all things. Just as the harvest returns the fruits of the earth to nourish and sustain us, so too does death return our energies to nature. Sera's passing is a profound loss — but it is also a part of that eternal cycle, a giving back, so that life may begin anew. Her spirit will nourish the roots of this community, and in time, her memory will help us grow stronger.”*

*“Sera may be gone from this world, but she will always be a part of Triboar. As we watch these flames rise to the heavens, let us send her spirit with our prayers, our love, and our gratitude. Farewell, Sera. May your journey be peaceful, and may you find the rest and peace you deserve.”*

It was a fine eulogy, and a true one, and the party heard it with divided attention — for Darathra had asked them, quietly, to watch the crowd. To watch for the one who grieved too much. D was still out there somewhere, and a secret lover at a public funeral is a difficult thing to hide. So they listened to the Lord Protector honor the dead with half an ear, and with the other half they read the faces of the living.

Few of the refugees had come. Among the handful who did was the tiefling family the party had helped — and the boy, @[Gregory](gregory), was not right. He stood through the rite visibly distraught, wound tight over something that seemed too large for a child at a stranger's funeral, and @[Amyl](amyl) and Corvyn felt the faint edge of it: his mind anxious, unclear, humming at the same frequency as the @[thing beneath the town](the-quiet-thing). His parents, asked gently about their road and their reasons, turned the questions aside as they always did. They were not refugees, they said again; they had come for their own purpose, something about a better future for their children. They would go no further than that. And the party let it lie, and filed the boy's distress away with everything else that did not yet add up.

There was another in the square who could not master himself. A man, off at the edge of the gathering, undone by the pyre in a way that drew the eye — and when he broke from the crowd and left, the party followed. He went to the groundskeeper's dwelling and shut himself inside. They knocked. @[Iratius](iratius) did the talking, with a pretext thin enough to see through: a cake to sell, a neighborly call. The door opened on a heavyset man built for outdoor work, a laborer's thick shoulders under a plain, earth-worn coat, a rough beard going gray in streaks, deep-set eyes red-rimmed and swollen in a weathered face — a man wrecked by grief, who gave his name as @[Darz](darz) and little else, and did not need to say the rest for the party to gather it. The care in the letters. The devastation on his face. The big, dirt-seamed hands of a groundskeeper, holding the door frame as though it were holding him up. Here, almost certainly, was D — not a murderer, not a Sharran, but a man who had loved Sera in secret and now had nowhere to put the loss of her.

They left him to his grief. @[Darathra](darathra), when they returned to her, asked them again to find the truth of Sera's death — and let fall that it might be part of something larger than one murder in a wood. She spoke of Shar, and of Sharrans, and admitted this was not the first time she had crossed the followers of the Lady of Loss. But something in it, she said, still did not add up. The pieces were on the board and they pointed in too many directions at once: a name no one knew, a corruption in the trees, a presence beneath the streets, a boy who felt wrong, a lover with nothing left, and a dream of a grave whose name could not be read. The party stood at a crossroads, and any road they chose would cost them the others.
