# XXI. Silver Fire
*In which a lady long absent is found, and something old is given back*

@audio: gray-dream fade=1
The surge took the western wall in the first hour, and it took it the way floods take bridges: all at once, after long patience. The dusk rose over the thorn-line like a tide standing on end; the unpickers came through in ranks and drank whole torch-rings at a swallow; the striders were among the platforms before the birds could stop singing, because there were no birds left to stop; and the tall census-taker crossed the wall for the first time, unhurried even now, and where it walked, the wardens' green ramparts died of nothing at all. The defense did what defenses do in the worst hour — it broke into islands, each fire its own last stand — and the party spent themselves along the collapsing line like a purse in a plague year: @[Iratius](iratius) a lighthouse of summoned flame; @[Bayne](bayne) a pale line in the dark, believed in; @[Tirig](tirig) everywhere the wall was worst; the seniors linked and burning through their art at rates no chart would survive.

The dusk reached the hazel grove in the second hour, and Berra was at the gate.

She had checked the wards three times that evening. She was still holding her ledger-satchel — they found it after, strap wound twice around her wrist, the way you carry a thing you mean to keep — and she set her warden's staff at the gap in the hurdles and she did not move. The record has assembled the minute from the accounts of those fighting toward her: the dark coming on at the gate not as shapes but as an argument, a pressure that unmade wards knot by knot; the custodian answering it knot by knot, relighting what it unpicked, her voice going through the warding-lines in order like a woman saying rosaries at speed; and behind her, past the hurdles, the dead man standing in his warded quiet with his fogged eyes open — and the mosslight rising under his skin, brighter and brighter, helpless, a lamp flaring behind a window at something it can see coming up the walk. She held the gate a minute. Perhaps a minute and some seconds. It was enough — the grove's inner wards took, the dark recoiled from the hazel line, the dead man was not taken — and it cost everything, and when @[Amyl](amyl) reached her, seconds and a whole life too late, the big man made a sound that nobody in the camps had heard from him in all this record, and the forest heard a gentle thing be loud at last. She had kept the master two years. She kept him one minute more. Both facts now belonged to the same sentence forever.

What followed followed fast. The line's islands were guttering; the eldest called the retreat to the Tree itself — the last border there is — and the retreat had begun, torch by failing torch, when every shape in the dark stopped, all together, and turned. Not toward the Tree. South. The way a congregation turns. And up through the camps, walking, came the silver.

@audio: silver-fire fade=4
She came through the wreck of the western wall with the fire already awake on her — not carried, worn, running on her shoulders and down her arms like dawn deciding not to wait for the hour — and where she walked, the surge unhappened. The unpickers burned off backward out of the air, unpicked. The striders' silences were found and emptied. The census-taker met her at the fallen thorn-line and was, for the first time in its patient existence, counted — and did not survive the counting. The defenders rallied in her wake without one order being given, because soldiers follow weather; and she crossed the whole terrible field to the eldest warden of the assembled circles, silver-haired and travel-gray and older than the city that missed her, and said, without preamble, the only introduction the night required: “Show me the door.”

---

What closing a door between worlds looks like, the record can only report from witnesses who could not look away. She stood at the dusk's frayed border where the clenched roots grayed, and she put the @[Silver Fire](silver-fire) in — not thrown, not spoken: poured, the silver leaving her the way a river leaves a lake, running the wound's edges like solder finding a seam. The dark resisted. The door did not want to be a wall again; the fartherness pressed back, and the silver bowed — and then the Tree came in. Every clenched root in the ring tightened as the silver braced it, wood and fire finding each other's grip, and for the first time in a whole spent season the oldest living thing was not fighting alone. The seam closed from the edges inward, dusk collapsing toward its own dark heart like water down a drain — and at the end there was a sound, one note, felt in the teeth and the roots and the held breath of every living thing in the country, the sound of an enormous door coming home into an enormous frame — and then noon. Ordinary, green, impossible noon, arriving in the root-country where it had not been in weeks, and the whole forest exhaling at once, and sixty seniors of the circles kneeling not in ceremony but because their legs had finished.

The party knew the silver. They had known it from the first heartbeat of her arrival and said nothing through the whole of the closing, holding the knowledge the way you hold your breath — because they had seen this fire once before: thinner, desperate, mixed with a dying woman's blood on the face of a stone under a town that no longer exists. It was @[Tirig](tirig) who finally asked, that evening, while the camps counted their dead — asked plainly, being Tirig: what is it, and why do we know it. And the silver-haired woman looked at her for a long moment with something terrible and patient moving behind her eyes, and said: “Later. The dead first. Both questions keep; the dead do not.”

@audio: none fade=8
The dead were eleven wardens and one custodian, and the camps buried them at the border they had held, in the first true noon, which the circles say is the best light for it. @[Amyl](amyl) carried Berra himself. And at her graveside there occurred the thing the camps would retell longest: the wardens brought the master — walked him from his ringed grove through the mended morning — and the dead man stood at the custodian's grave with the party and the circles, fogged eyes open, the mosslight faint and steady under his skin; and Amyl stood beside him; and for the length of the rites, the raised man and the man who had raised him kept her together. @[Saeron](saeron) moved down the line of the eleven and said the words over each grave — the language of endurance again, patient, unhurried, one at a time, all of them — and the wardens of the circles, who did not know the rite, stood for the whole of it anyway in the new noon light. Nobody who saw it called it abomination. Several who had, at a trial not long before, were seen to stand nearer to him than they strictly needed to.

The reckonings came the following day, at the pace the dead had bought them. Who she was, the camps had assembled by breakfast — the name the party had heard once through a mask; the empty tower; the statues by the river — Alustriel. @[Alustriel Silverhand](alustriel), High Lady of @[Silverymoon](silverymoon) for centuries and absent from it for years, standing in road-dust at the foot of the Grandfather Tree. She addressed the absence exactly once, to the eldest, in the party's hearing: “I know what the city says. The city has its Marshal, and the war is not at the city. It is at the lights — and someone is making the world into a place where the old lights fail. A stone under a market town, cut on purpose. A bridge, detuned a string a week. And this —” she looked down the healing root-country — “which nobody cut at all. Doors are beginning to open on their own, where the fabric is oldest. That is what their work elsewhere is buying, whether they intend every purchase or not. I stopped answering letters because I have been going where the letters are not.”

She asked them for Triboar, and they gave it to her whole — the stone, the couplings, the rites, the glass, the night — and she listened the way deep water listens, asking almost nothing. And then, at the end of it, in a voice that had rehearsed being steady across several hundred miles: “There was a woman of the town's Twelve. @[Sera](sera). Tell me about Sera.” Their faces answered before anyone could arrange them. Alustriel closed her eyes for exactly as long as it takes a candle to steady. “All of it,” she said. “Leave nothing out because it is cruel.”

They left nothing out: the murder in the autumn dressed as a lovers' tragedy; the engineering under it, learned too late — a meeting manufactured, a grieving pawn aimed like a quarrel, a fiend's particular interest in one particular soul; and the cave — Tirig told this part, having been nearest — the @[mooring stone](mooring-stones) drinking its rites, and a dying woman's blood striking it, and the silver, thin and desperate, cracking through the black like dawn through a frozen pond, throwing the working out of true. Alustriel did not move for a long time. “She was mine,” she said at last, simply. “My daughter. One of many; and every one of them carries what I carry, whether they will or no. You watched me spend a morning's worth of it on that door. Mystra's own fire, born in my blood and passed to my children's — and it is one of the few fires under any sky that can close a door between worlds. She spent all of hers in one breath, untrained, unasked, on pure instinct, to spoil a working she could not have named.” The silence did the arithmetic, and it was @[Saeron](saeron) who finally said it, gently, because someone had to: “Then she wasn't killed for what she knew. She was killed for what her blood could close.” “Yes,” said @[Alustriel Silverhand](alustriel). “My daughter was murdered by planners, for a door she never knew she was.”

And then, because they had earned it twice over, she gave them the name. “The far side of every such door is one country,” she said. “You have bled at two of its thresholds now, so hear it once, plainly, from someone who has stood in it: @[the Shadowfell](the-gray-country). The world's own shadow — the gray twin of everything, where lost light settles and grief pools like groundwater. Every door they cut, and every door that opens worn, opens there. Keep the name. You will need it before this is done, and I would rather you had it from me than from something on the far side of a threshold.”

@unlock: the-grandfather-tree.4
She healed @[the Tree](the-grandfather-tree) that afternoon — walked the great blackened root from its far end inward, barefoot, one hand trailing the burned crown, and behind her hand the last of the sickness simply ended, the spent wood arriving back into its color, the canopy turning green in a spreading ring like a sky changing its mind — and she came off the far end pale as her hair and declined every arm offered. “I can close what opens,” she said. “I can heal what the holding cost. I cannot yet reach the ones making the world a place where such doors open. Do not mistake this good day for the war.”

---

That evening, under the healed canopy, she gathered the seven of them to a small fire apart, and paid the Tree's other debts.

“I cannot tell you what the seven of you are,” she said. “I am not certain the word exists yet. I can tell you what I feel standing near you, because I have felt bound companies before — twice, in a long life — and never seven at once: you ring like one bell struck seven ways. And I can tell you this, which I have carried for years the way you carry a missing tooth: there is a hole in the world where a name should be. I do not know whose. I only know that every one of you stands nearer to that hole than anyone I have ever met — the @[Tree has been restless](the-grandfather-tree) since you arrived, the way old things are restless around unfinished business. It is trying to give you something. I am only going to lend it a voice. Hold still.”

@audio: gray-dream fade=4
What happened then, each of them described afterward the same way, and each apologized for the description: for the space of perhaps ten heartbeats, they remembered. Not learned — remembered, the way you remember a house you lived in: that @[the gray is not a dream](the-gray-country). That it is a place — a shore, with a weather and a horizon and a patience — and that they have stood on it, all seven of them, truly, together, since before any of them could hold a spoon. That the cold they had fought all season at the wound's edge and the cold of that shore are one cold; the fartherness and the gray share a country. And the eighth figure — for ten heartbeats they saw her. A woman, standing a little apart on the dark sand, the way she has always stood; and every one of them, separately, without a word exchanged, arrived at the same certainty and carried it up out of the remembering like a stone from a riverbed: they knew her. The woman of the silent showings — the threshold night they had watched from a crypt's floor, the festival of the graves — her. The same. Then it receded — like tide, like waking — and left them the knowing without the remembering, which is the difference between a map and a country. @[Sov](sov) sat with his fists closed on his knees and did not trust himself to speak. Somewhere far overhead, in the healed canopy, something settled its wings.

“And one of you,” @[Alustriel](alustriel) said, into that silence, “is owed a memory of his own. The Tree heard it at your trial — the why of you, carried off. It kept what reached its roots. Come here, warden.”

@[Amyl](amyl) knelt at the fire, grief-hollowed and huge, and she put her palm to his forehead the way you test a child for fever, and gave him back a night. Rain, first — he said it aloud as it arrived, in pieces — night rain through canopy; grave soil under his nails; his master three days dead and the grief in him speaking words of a working he had never learned, had no right to, could not afterward account for. And the working answering — not completing under his hands but being completed, from below, through the roots, by @[something green and vast and patient](the-quiet-thing) that lifted the dead man the way you lift a lamp back onto its hook. “I did not do it alone,” he said, and his voice went somewhere it had never been in front of the party. “I never did it alone. It heard me grieving and it — helped. It helped me.” And then the memory reached the place where the why lived — the showing, the question it had asked him, the thing he had said yes to — and frayed, precisely there, like a rope cut clean. Alustriel took her hand away gently. “The Tree's memory ends at its roots' reach,” she said. “The rest of your why is being kept somewhere else. And understand me, warden, because I have some standing in this matter: memory is never destroyed. It is only ever kept.”

It was later that night, with the fire low, that @[Amyl](amyl) gave the party the rest of the frame he had carried alone — the part he had always had, laid down now beside the piece the Tree had returned, so that everyone could see the shape of what was missing. His master had died on a mission. The two of them, ranging far out for the circle, on an errand Amyl could still recite the supply list of — and then a single hour that went wrong in a way his memory refuses at the edges, and his master dead, and Amyl alone with him three days' walk from anywhere. What he had never had, until tonight, was the night itself: he remembered burying grief-mad in the rain, and he remembered — next, with nothing between — his master standing, alive after the fashion he is alive, and Amyl kneeling in the turned soil of a grave that had been opened from below. The Circle's wardens found them so, days later. They took custody of the raised man, and they took Amyl's cords, and they wrote novice under watch and suspected Blight-work in him from that morning to this one — and he had never once been able to tell them whether they were wrong. “Until tonight,” he said, turning the little mushroom cap over in the firelight, “I could not even tell you whether I had done it. Now I know that I asked — and that something heard me, and helped, and asked me something back. What I still do not know is what it asked. And what I promised.” Nobody offered a comfort, because the fire had already heard Alustriel say the only true one: whatever he had promised, it was being kept.

The council that followed was brief, because the map had grown opinionated. The @[crystal](the-heart-of-winters-veil) pulled north; @[Volcaris](volcaris), done arguing at last, pointed the same way, two rulings on one bearing; and @[Alustriel](alustriel) studied the alignment, and the woman the crystal pulled, rather longer than she had studied the door. “The Spine,” she said. “Then hear a traveler's report. The North past the moors has answered to a new hand these two years — it posts its law at the fords and signs in virtues, and it does not love walkers it has not licensed. Go as strangers, not as heroes; heroes are inventoried up there. As for me — there is a bridge that needs its lady, and a city that deserves better than my silence. If you find another of the old lights failing, send no letters. Light a fire on the nearest height. I will know it from any distance you can imagine, and from several you cannot.”

They left the @[Grandfather Tree](the-grandfather-tree) two mornings later, under a canopy so green it made the memory of the long dusk seem like slander. The circles turned out to see them off — which is to say, to see @[Amyl](amyl) off. The eldest took his huge hand in both of hers at the bowl's rim and said, “The chair waits. Consent when you are ready” — and then she gave him the other thing, and the record watched him take it: Berra's ledgers, two years of faithful columns, strap wound twice. “She would want her columns finished,” the eldest said. Neth walked out of the camps at his shoulder with a pack of cuttings and the fixed expression of a woman who had re-argued her assignment three times and won thrice; and the company — eight, and nobody counted otherwise — took the road north, into the young winter, toward the mountains at the top of the world.
