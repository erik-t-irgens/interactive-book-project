# Interlude — Before the Harvest
*Being an accounting of what the party knew, what they suspected, and what still had no shape at all — set down in the small hours before the festival*

@audio: before-the-harvest fade=8
There were perhaps four hours between the fire and the dawn, and the party spent them the only way left to spend anything: taking stock.

They gathered in Darathra's map-room with the smoke still bitter in their clothes, and they emptied their packs and their pockets and their memories onto her great table, and looked at what a season of blood had bought them. A broken pendant, Shar's mark on one face and a stranger sign etched into the other. Two notes in a firm, unhurried hand, and a third in a blunter one. A ledger they could not read and a silver instrument that played a song they could not place. A @[crystal that leaned north](the-heart-of-winters-veil) in a closed fist. Seven numbered love letters, and the eighth that answered them. And around the table, eight people who had woken in this town as strangers with matching holes in their memories, which was — they had slowly stopped pretending otherwise — the strangest object in the room.

Laid out like that, in the lamplight, the season stopped looking like chaos. It looked like three braids of one rope.

---

The first braid was the murder, and the machine behind it.

@[Sera](sera)'s death had never been a crime of passion; it was ritual, confessed as ritual by the woman who did it, done for a purpose the doer herself served without understanding. But the killing was only the last tooth of a long gear-train, and the party could now count the teeth. Her love affair had been manufactured — @[Hyuth](hyuth) confessed to that much before his own tongue seized: the meeting arranged, the romance engineered, on instructions he could not name. Her protection had been altered — someone had tampered with the Twelve's rotations in the days before she walked into the wood alone, and @[Merris Thulane](merris) had burned the records rather than let the tampering be read. And the order beneath all of it had arrived in writing, twice, signed by a virtue: @[Prepare for Harvest](the-harvest). The light blinds the veil. —Verity.

There was one more tooth the party could see and could not yet prove. Hyuth's whole trade, in that gilded cellar, had been assurances — protection packages, guard rotations, quiet guarantees, the rearranging of who stood watch and where, sold as a product to the frightened rich. Someone had rearranged Sera's watch before she died. The party could not prove the two facts touched. They could not stop noticing them, either. A fixer who sells altered rotations; a traitor who burns rotation records; a paladin of the Twelve dead on a night her rotation was altered. If those were three separate stories, coincidence was working very hard that season.

What the machine had been for — why this woman, why by ritual, why her death mattered enough to spend a fiend's fixer and a council traitor and a cult's knife on — the party could not say. They knew only what they had seen with their own eyes: that where Sera died the forest thrived; that her dried blood had burned silver on a Sharran amulet and pushed back a darkness that nothing else touched; that the mirror had shown all of them, through Nik's eyes, a light in her that was never wholly hers to give. Whatever Sera had been, her killers had known it, and her husband had not, and neither had she.

---

The second braid ran north, and it was made of cold.

An orc had walked out of the far North before any of this began, unarmed and unresisting, wearing frost that no season explained — bound by a geas laid on him by something of winter, sent south to find a thing he could not name. Around his cell, an old fey tongue whispered on the frost, grieving, begging forgiveness of a lady beneath the ice. The thing he was sent to find, he paid in pain to say, was in mortal hands — and belonged to a white forest with two skies, beyond a cave mouth in the uttermost North. And the Hollow Tusks who came for him through a door no one should have known — strangers to him, silent about their orders — carried a note from a second virtue: Deliver the frost-bound to his purpose, and let the season's rite conclude unhindered. —Conviction.

Every strand of that braid ran through @[Tirig](tirig). She had come out of the North with no memory of the road; the frost thickened when she neared his cell; the pull she felt was a hand on her shoulder; the crystal she had carried out of the white leaned north in her fist, and in no one else's; and some of the party had seen, in a black mirror underground, a forest of silver trees under two skies with a heart of crystal at its center — an image the orc had now confirmed as a real place, in words that cost him frost to speak. The white had let her keep exactly one thing, and the one thing pointed home. She had stopped, by the end of that night, pretending its weight was ordinary.

---

The third braid ran straight down.

Beneath Triboar something vast and frightened had been listening since the first night — the @[presence](the-quiet-thing) that touched Amyl and Corvyn through the spores, that was louder in one small tiefling boy than in anyone living, that had gone silent in the between-place by the cave and diminished when the @[black stone](mooring-stones) fell dormant. The wood's sickness had flowed from that stone: ichor growing out of living things, beasts unmade into weapons, a corruption anchored by a working the party had wounded but not understood — a stone custom-tuned, the reading had said, to a vessel. The Sharrans had built a mirror-hall to feed on Loss and Pain and Oblivion in the deep of the forest. And now there were tunnels under the town itself, abandoned by every map and used by someone all the same: the sluice stood open in the Tower's roots, breathing cold, and the rumble that had rolled beneath the whole night was not beneath them anymore. It was down that throat. Ahead.

And the word Harvest lay across this braid and the first one like a brand. Verity had ordered someone to prepare for it. Conviction had called it the season's rite and wanted it unhindered. The town, hanging its lanterns on the surviving posts, meant by the same word only bread and beer and the turning of the year — and dawn was coming, and with it High Harvestide, and the party alone in all of Triboar suspected that when the devout wrote Harvest they were not talking about wheat.

---

And through all three braids ran a fourth thread that belonged to no one but the eight of them, and it was gray.

The matching amnesia. The recognition that needed no introductions. The @[tune](the-tune) Corvyn played that first night that every one of them somehow knew, and that @[Aelendir](aelendir) completed in a crowded hall to a silence like held breath. The colorless dream and its circle of silhouettes — six, then eight, growing exactly as the company grew — and the far figure that never came closer and never turned away. The bright country with the grave, and @[the girl](the-girl-at-the-grave), and the name that would not be read. The visions in the black mirror, each kept private, each too personal to be chance. None of it connected to Sharrans or schedules or frost in any way the party could draw on Darathra's table. And yet one person had spoken of it as though it were the most legible thing in the room. The knot that binds you, the shadowless bartender had said, was tied with defter hands than mine. Whatever their gray thread was, @[Veyran](veyran) had recognized it on sight — which meant it was a kind of thing, a thing with other examples, a thing a creature like him had seen before. That thought was not comforting. They kept it anyway.

---

And when the braids were laid side by side, the last discovery was time.

The season had a schedule. Sera was killed as the festival approached, on a watch that had been quietly altered, with Prepare for Harvest already written in someone's unhurried hand. The masquerade was thrown across the festival's final nights — and Darathra had suspected from the first gilt invitation that it wanted her out of her Tower, which was the whole reason a shapeshifter wore her face to the ball while the Lord Protector stayed home with her keys. Hyuth confessed, when his tongue allowed it, that he had been instructed to keep the party occupied on that last night — knowing something would happen at the Tower, never trusted with what. The fire was set under cover of the masquerade's dying hours; the sluice was opened while the records still smoked; and the rite the raiders served was ordered to conclude unhindered at the season's hinge — on High Harvestide itself, the oldest turning-day of the year, when even the town's grandmothers said the world wore thin. None of these things had happened near each other. All of them had happened on time.

And one small wrongness from the tasting now read entirely differently. Hyuth had made a point of announcing @[Darathra](darathra) to his chosen guests — calling out the Lord Protector's presence by name, to the whole cellar — and the party had taken it for a rich man's needling. Set against the timetable, it looked like something else: confirmation. Someone, somewhere, needed to know that Darathra was at Boar's Rest before the night's other business could begin, and Hyuth had duly confirmed it — and the confirmation had been false, because the woman behind the mask was @[Tirig](tirig). The enemy's clockwork had run that whole night on a wrong assumption of the party's own accidental making. Had Darathra been where the schedule put her, there would have been no blade at the choke-point when the sluice ground open, no Mistwarden below to know her own keys. The party had jammed a gear in a machine they had not yet known existed — which told them two things at once: that the machine could be jammed, and that next time, it would count its Darathras more carefully.

Two anomalies, last of all — the two facts that refused to sit in any single braid, and so bound them all together. The black mirror in the deep was a Sharran engine: fed on three bound lives, tethered through the stone toward the anchor, enemy machinery to its core. And yet when the party stood before it, it had set aside its business and shown each of them something true, and personal, and their own — prophecy, cut to the measure of eight strangers, from a device built by their enemies. Enemy machinery had recognized them. And the buried presence beneath the town — afraid since the first night, fleeing something it had never named — had changed the day the stone fell dormant: quieter, dimmer, its terror turned down like a lamp, as though its fear and the enemy's working were coupled somewhere out of sight. The party could not say what either fact meant. But both of them said the same thing in different tongues: the threads knew each other. The wood, the town, the masquerade, the cells, the deep — not five stories, but one system, touching itself everywhere.

*Coincidence does not keep a calendar. Somewhere behind the virtue-names there was a score, and every figure in Triboar — the willing like @[Merris](merris), the bound like Hyuth, the spent like the one-handed Cultist, the ignorant like @[Darz](darz), the innocent like the town itself — had played their bar of it on cue. The party had spent the season reading events one at a time, as they arrived. Laid together on Darathra's table, they read as a timetable. And every line of it converged on a single square of the calendar, which was today.*

---

So, in the last hour before dawn, the party did the accounting that mattered most: someone must have answers. They went down the list of everyone who might, and the list itself turned out to be the discovery.

@[Hyuth](hyuth) knew — who instructed him, why Sera, what the assurances truly bought — and Hyuth's tongue seized in his throat at the approach of every question that counted. @[Gorvach](gorvach) knew what sent him and what it wanted, and paid frost for every syllable, surfacing for a minute at a time before winter took him back. Tovric Hale had seen enough to be worth silencing, and had no voice left to tell it with, only a splinter and soft wood. The @[Silent Ledger](the-silent-ledger) surely held the machine's own records — routes, names, the architecture of the assurances — and it was written in a cipher the party could not crack; the one person who had offered to decode it, the veiled courier @[Aevren](aevren), had last been seen at a masquerade that had since proven to be mostly illusion, and no one knew where she slept. Merris knew everything a traitor knows, and Merris was gone down the sluice ahead of them, into the dark, toward whatever paid her. @[Veyran](veyran) knew the most of anyone — he had all but said so, smiling — and Veyran had declared himself their enemy on his way out the door. The virtue-names knew, and were names on notes. The thing below knew, perhaps, in whatever way a buried mountain knows things, and it could only be asked in person, down the throat of the earth, where a child's bare footprints led.

*And @[Darz](darz) — poor Darz, the truest mourner in the whole affair — might know more than anyone had thought to ask him. His romance had been engineered, which meant someone had carried the letters, arranged the chance meetings, built the beautiful accident of his love with @[Sera](sera) piece by piece — and Darz had lived inside that construction for months without knowing it had walls. He had seen the couriers. He had met the go-betweens. He held, without knowing he held it, a witness's memory of the machine's own hands at work. And Darz had fled the party in confusion and grief days ago, spooked by a stranger who knew his dead love's name, and was out there still, as far as anyone knew — alone, in the corrupted forest, in the dark, at the edge of a town about to celebrate a festival that two separate virtues had ordered to proceed unhindered. Every road on the table led to someone who could not, would not, or should not speak. The party sat with that for a long moment, in the lamplight, with the evidence spread before them. The silence around this mystery was not an accident of the case. The silence was the design.*

Then the first gray light came up over the Evermoors, and somewhere in the town below a baker who had lost her nephew in the night lit her ovens anyway, because the festival was today and bread does not care. The lanterns swayed on the surviving posts. The sluice breathed its cold at the bottom of the Tower. And the party rose from the table, and armed themselves, and went down to the Harvest.
