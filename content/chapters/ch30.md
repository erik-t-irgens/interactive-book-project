# XXX. The Second Hand
*In which a voice returns, and the record does not interrupt it*

@audio: fundament-doubled fade=8
There is too much to build. I begin there because it is where I always begin: the sentence is my morning prayer, my ledger's first line, the hand I put out to each day to pull myself up into it. A year ago it was a hope. This morning it is a schedule.

The council met at the second bell, in the long room above the grain-exchange, because I have never seen the use of a hall you cannot heat. Twelve chairs, six of them mattering; a table older than the doctrine that sits at it; and windows that hold both lights at once at that hour — the pale day and the deep one — which no one who lives here looks up at anymore, and which I still look up at every single time. I keep the minutes myself. Someone must, and there is no better seat in any room than the one everyone forgets.

@[The Enforcer](verity) stood to report, and reported a defeat, and did it the way I would have a defeat reported: dates, numbers, no adjectives. The working at the river town is broken. The instrument is glass on a cellar floor. The intake is lost — not delayed; lost — and the seven interferers stood in the wreckage of a season's work and were, by every account we hold, not even winded. He sat down. I wrote it all in a fair hand. A minute-book that flinches is not a minute-book.

Then the room did what rooms do. The Priest called it a pruning and had scripture ready before the word finished landing; the Diplomat began drafting the version the coast will hear before the true one gets its boots on; the Seeker gave us four unasked minutes on the bridge, which proceeds; the Warrior wanted to march on a moor town in midwinter, which I love him for and will never permit; and the Oracle, asked directly what the seven will do next, said, “They will come here,” which is accurate, useless, and exactly what I have learned to expect from accuracy. I asked my three small questions. I thanked everyone twice. A meeting is a room you build fresh every morning out of other people's weather, and I am a builder; it has never once felt like a chore.

Here is the year as I keep it, in the privacy of minutes nobody reads. The market town: delivered. The Tree: denied — the wood-priests will keep their circle, and I have stopped spending on that door; a good mason knows which stones refuse the wall. And the river town: seen. I chose that word with care and I will keep it. Not lost. Seen. For a year we worked behind weather and paper, and now seven strangers have put their hands clean through the paper, and the age of our anonymity is over. Good. Anonymity was falsework. What we are building was always going to have to stand in the open at the end.

I have read everything we hold on them, twice. Capable — more than capable; the river town retires any argument that the Tree was luck. Sentimental — they spend themselves on strangers at ruinous rates and call it economy. And expensive to their enemies in exactly the proportion that their enemies insist on remaining enemies. In the margin of their file I have written the only strategic note that matters: people who will not spend anyone can always, always be brought to a table. You cannot negotiate with a mercenary — there is nothing he wants that cannot be outbid. But the sentimental want everyone alive. I am the only party to this war who can offer that. I intend, at the proper hour, to be taken up on it.

So I wrote to them. Four drafts, which I record because honesty about small vanities is a discipline like any other: the first three were explanations, and one does not explain to people who have just beaten one. One welcomes them. A greeting and a promise, which between them say everything that is true — that we saw them; that we do not hide; and that the work proceeds on schedule regardless of how anyone feels about it.

I passed the page up the table for the @[Enforcer](verity)'s hand. He read it twice and looked at me down the length of the wood — he knows whose sentences these are; he is not a stupid man, whatever the seven make of his prose — and I said: it should carry your title. You are the one they beat. Let their first letter from this house teach them the thing about us that ought genuinely to frighten them: we do not hide our defeats. We enter them, and reconcile them, and proceed. He signed in full, because we always sign in full. A title is a promise. An initial is an escape hatch.

A novice carried it down to the shard-room, where the pieces of every broken working stand in their velvet like relics — because glass remembers being one glass, and a message given to any of it arrives at all of it. I am told our pane fogged from this side, like a breath. I find the mechanism beautiful. Most of what we do is beautiful, if you stand where I stand. That nobody else will stand here until the work is finished is the tax on the view, and I pay it without complaint, and I keep the receipts.

Because — I will say it here, in the one book that cannot repeat me — everything they believe they interrupted is a rescue. I have stood where nothing rescues. My people are born owing, and die owing, and are returned to the debt without once being asked, and the wheel does not even hate us; hatred would at least be attention. What we build here ends that. Not for me — I stopped pricing my own share a long time ago — for all of them; for gray-eyed children not yet born who will never know what was ended for them, which is the entire point of ending a thing. There is a cost. I have never pretended otherwise in these pages. I keep the cost in its own column, in my own hand, and when the work is finished I will carry that column to whatever judgment wants it, and I will not append one word of defense.

The council rose at dusk. I stayed, as I stay, with the candles and the two lights in the window, and I said the sentence aloud to the empty room — the mason's habit, tapping a joint to hear whether it rings: “They are being delivered, not taken.” It rang. It rings a little differently each season. I attribute that to the room.

And the candle did the thing it has taken to doing, some nights, lately: the edge of the flame went gray for one breath — colorless, like light remembering something — and then it was only fire again. I have a rule about the gray. I made the rule a long time ago, with my eyes open, for reasons I audited thoroughly at the time and see no profit in auditing again, and the rule is: it means nothing. A rule is not a fact. A rule is a decision, and decisions must be renewed, and I renew this one whenever it asks — which is more often, lately, than I find worth recording. Dreams are the mind sweeping its floor. Flames are air. I do not grant it meaning. Meaning is granted to what serves the work, and the gray has never once lifted a stone.

@audio: fundament-doubled fade=4
I blew the candles out myself. I always do. It is the one task I will not delegate, though I could not tell you why, and I distrust that in me, and I have written it down anyway, because a true minute-book keeps even its keeper. Tomorrow: the intake schedule, the liaisons' invoice, the bridge. There is too much to build.

@audio: none fade=6
— You have heard this voice before. It opened this book. I am not going to introduce her; she has been introducing herself one margin at a time since the second chapter, and she would consider an introduction a waste of a page. I will say only this, and then get out of her way, which is more than I ever managed at our table: I costed writing her as a monster. It would have been cheaper. It would also have been a lie.
