# I. Shadows Beneath Triboar
*In which five strangers meet as though for the second time*

@audio: triboar-hearth fade=6
Triboar was restless that autumn, and it had reason to be.

The town sat where two great roads crossed at the edge of the civilized North, a way-stop for caravans and cattle drovers, too stubborn to be a village and too rough to be a city. It had weathered orc-raids and hard winters and the slow indifference of the wider world, and it wore that history plainly, in timber gone gray and cobbles worn smooth. But this season the trouble came not from without but from the east, and it came on foot. Refugees had begun to arrive — dwarves from the Nether Mountains, elves from the deep forests, humans from towns whose names meant nothing here — all of them carrying the same hollow look and the same useless answer when asked what they fled. They did not know. Only that the land behind them was dying, and that whatever killed it had no face to point to.

So the market was crowded and the tempers were short, and the harvest festival that should have been a comfort had become one more strain on a town with too little to go around. Missing livestock. Missing coin. Missing people, now and then, which the sensible folk blamed on the newcomers and the newcomers blamed on the dark they had run from. Not all who came to @[Triboar](triboar) that season were fleeing, though the town made no such distinction; some were only passing through, bound elsewhere on errands of their own, and had stopped for reasons they did not fully understand themselves. Into this walked five strangers, on the same day, by five different roads — and though not one of them had arranged it, not one of them was entirely surprised to find the others there.

---

At the Northshield Inn the fire was warm and the welcome was not. @[Mishka](mishka) kept the place with a half-orc's shoulders and a veteran's eye, and half the room feared her while the other half merely respected her, which suited her fine. It was in her cellar that the first of them was already at work.

*@[Amyl Rachis](amyl) crouched among the crates where the stone floor gave way to a narrow burrow — crouched being the only posture the cellar allowed him, for Amyl stood over seven and a half feet when anything let him, a firbolg built like a broad-set human scaled up past argument, deep olive skin warmed with an undertone of orange, a wild and unkempt rust-red mane and beard framing a face far gentler than the frame it sat on. He studied the thing that should not have been growing there. Pale mushrooms clung to the lip of the hole, glowing faintly blue and green in the dark, and his broad hands moved among them with the care of someone who has spent a life listening to growing things. A rat had gone down that hole — he had watched it go — and its eyes had caught the light wrongly, too bright, too aware. He pinched free a single cap and held it to what little light there was. It was not myconid. It was not anything he had a name for. But it was part of something, the way a fingertip is part of a hand, and the something was near.*

Upstairs, the common room went about its evening. It was Mishka's voice that fetched Amyl back to it — calling down the cellar stairs to ask whether he had found the source of her rat problem yet, the task she had set him and the reason he was down among her crates at all. Amyl pocketed the pale cap and climbed up into the warmth and the noise, ducking the lintel, easing between tables with the murmured apologies of a man who has spent his whole life feeling like furniture rearranged into a crowd's way — and took a long look at the evening he surfaced into. @[Iratius](iratius) sat alone with his fists on the table and a prayer he could not finish behind his teeth — a tiefling the deep red-brown of old embers, black horns sweeping back from his brow in ridged pairs, dark-maned and dark-bearded, sitting a tavern bench in scarred layered armor like a man who had never once been persuaded that any war was over. A tiefling, in a town that had no love for tieflings, and no patience for one who brooded. When a bearded local made the mistake of saying so, the fire behind Iratius's eyes was answer enough; and Mishka's voice cut across the room before it could become anything worse, promising the man he would be scrubbing his own blood from her floor if he pressed it. He did not press it.

A gust from the door scattered a raven's feather across the boards, and @[Sov Thimron](sov) stooped to gather it as he came in from the cold. There was something not quite placeable in him — a grayness to his coloring, like a man seen by moonlight in the middle of the day; ears that came very slightly to points; dark hair cropped close at the sides — and, winding up out of his collar and over jaw and brow, a lattice of fine pale tattoos in tight geometric lines, patterns that looked less drawn than written, in an alphabet no one present could have read. No one could have named the origin of any of it, least of all Sov himself. He carried a spiked mace at his belt and a @[six-sided shield](sovs-shield) across his back blazoned with a set of balanced scales, and he wore the weariness of a man well acquainted with endings and unsurprised by them. He asked Mishka for something strong, and then — with a glance at the tiefling nursing his temper in the corner — asked for a second, and set it down before Iratius without a word. It was the sort of small kindness that starts things. The two fell to talking, and found in each other a familiarity neither could account for, as though they were resuming a conversation rather than beginning one.

In another corner, perched on a stool built for longer legs, a gnome coaxed a low, mournful melody from a violin — @[Corvyn](corvyn), a small neat figure in a traveler's brown jacket, tousled chestnut hair above great pointed ears, hazel eyes bright with a showman's mischief that the tune itself kept quietly contradicting. The notes drifted through the room like smoke. A woman leaned near to ask what the song was called. His own, he told her — though even as he said it, a shadow of doubt crossed his face, because it felt older than he was, as if he had not written it so much as remembered it. And here was the strange thing: around the room, the others stilled. Amyl, come up from the cellar, paused mid-thought. Sov's fingers went quiet on his cup. Even Iratius's scowl flickered into something like recognition. Four strangers, and @[one tune](the-tune), and every one of them somehow knew how it went. The fifth was upstairs still, sleeping off a journey she could not remember — and the tune, though none of them could have said how, was waiting for her too.

She had arrived not on her own feet but carried on another's. @[Tirig](tirig) woke in a patched bed upstairs with no memory of the journey. She was a daughter of the far North to look at — winter-pale, a loose fall of white-gold hair, and eyes of a blue so light they seemed lit from somewhere behind the ice of them — dressed for hard country in ranger's leathers beneath a deep red hooded cloak, a bow and a worn wooden flute among her few effects. And kept closest of all, on a cord, a @[small colorless crystal](the-heart-of-winters-veil) that she found her hand checking for before she was fully awake, without being able to say why it should be the first thing she reached for. The last thing she recalled was the cold wind of the far North cutting through furs, and now here was warmth, and a low ceiling, and a stern-eyed dwarf watching over her. @[Gurpnik](nik), he called himself; Nik to those who knew him. He had found her collapsed in the wood at @[Gwaeron's Rest](gwaerons-rest), west of the town, with no wound on her and no sign of struggle — simply fallen, as though set down there by something that had finished with her. He served @[the Twelve](the-twelve), the council that kept Triboar standing, and the town needed hands. He offered his own to help her up. She took it, and together they came down into the room where the others waited without knowing they waited.

@audio: gray-dream fade=1
And then, for a heartbeat, the world went out.

The inn drained of color and sound. The fire, the murmur, the smell of ale — gone, all at once, and in their place a monotonous gray, cold and horizonless. Only the five of them remained, each rendered in stark silhouette, each able to see the others plainly across that impossible distance, as if they alone were real and everything else had been a painting someone had stepped away from. None of them moved. None of them had time to. As swiftly as it came, the gray released them, and the inn rushed back — warm, loud, wholly unaware — and left five strangers staring at one another with the same question and no words to ask it.

@audio: triboar-hearth fade=2
The question would have to wait. Shouting rose from the street, and the smell of pitch-smoke: a mob had gathered before the @[Tower of the Lord Protector](the-lords-tower), torches high, voices raised over a rumor. The Tower was the tallest thing in Triboar — it commanded the town the way a mast commands a ship, banners flanking its great door and stirring in the torch-draft — and the crowd churned at the foot of it, small against all that stone. There was an orc in the cells, they said. They wanted it dead. High above, onto the balcony, stepped @[Darathra Shendrel](darathra) — Lord Protector of Triboar, an office the town bestows by election, and held by her on the strength of a good faith most of that crowd had freely given her. She stood almost too far up to make out. But her voice came down like something dropped from that height, booming across the square, and it told them flatly and with the authority of long practice that there was no such prisoner, and that they would do more harm with rumor than the truth ever could. Slowly, grudgingly, the crowd came apart and drifted home.

*Her eye fell on the five of them where they stood at the edge of it, marked as outsiders by their very bearing. Triboar was wary of outsiders just now, she said — but a wary town was also a town with too much to do and too few to do it. If they meant to stay, they could make themselves useful. A guard had gone missing. Children had gone missing. The festival loomed and the granaries were watched like a wound. She did not much care which they chose. She cared that they chose something. The five exchanged looks, strangers still, until it was Corvyn who spoke the thing the rest were feeling: children first.*

---

Their path led them to Ransor's Open Road — because that, the refugees said, was where the missing children had last been seen, and where no searching had found them since. The place was a ruined hall that had died the day a great boulder came through its roof and never left: only three of its faces still stood, the floors of the second and third stories hanging on in broken shelves except where the stone had punched through them, and in the middle of the ground floor — spreading out past the wall into the yard itself — a gaping hole opened into the dark below. They tested its depth as best they could, and the answers that came back up were a long fall softened into a slope: a decline of settled rubble, steep but slideable, going down further than their light. Getting down would be easy. Getting back up, they suspected — rightly, as the night would prove — would be another matter.

They went carefully. The upper floor could be reached only by a single beam left intact across the wound of the building, and it was Corvyn who crossed it — one careful foot in front of the other over the open drop — to reach the splintered shelter behind it, where a small tiefling girl was hiding. @[Kirsten](the-tiefling-family). He coaxed her out with a gentle word and a lowered instrument. She trusted them enough, with Iratius near, to point one trembling hand at the dark below, where her brother had gone. Then she bolted for the refugee tents and the safety of home.

@audio: undergrave-drone fade=5
Down they went, into a dark that pressed close and cold. Sov murmured a spell to see what lay ahead and his expression tightened at what it told him: the dead were down here, or something near enough to it. And then the light found them — rats, a writhing carpet of them, eyes aglow and limbs moving with a wrongness that turned the stomach, alive and not-alive at once, massed between the party and the small still shape beyond.

Amyl did not draw a weapon. He drew breath. The mushroom was still in his pocket, and he lifted it now and opened himself to it as he had in the cellar, letting the spores carry him outward into the minds of the swarm — and staggered under what came back. Fear. Not his, and not aimed at him. The rats were not hunting. They were fleeing, and the terror that drove them was of something deeper still, something further down and further east, and it was vast, and it was old, and it was afraid. Corvyn breathed the spores too, and shared the link, and for a moment the two of them stood inside the same enormous dread — a @[presence beneath the world](the-quiet-thing), anxious as a held breath, close as a heartbeat under the floor.

The rats stilled at Amyl's silent word. Then, as one, they broke and poured away into the deeper dark — not driven off by the party, but recoiling from whatever it was they all could suddenly feel. Beyond them lay the boy. @[Gregory](gregory). Pale, and cold, and terribly still.

Iratius went to his knees and pressed a hand to the small chest. Sov spoke low words over him. And slowly — mercifully — the boy's eyes fluttered and opened, and he clung to Iratius's arm and let himself be lifted, trembling but alive. They carried him up out of the pit, hauling one another over the broken slope, until the cold clean air of evening met them at the top and Gregory stumbled away toward the tents, breathless thanks trailing behind him.

The others turned to go. But Amyl did not, and Corvyn did not — not right away. @[The presence](the-quiet-thing) was still there, under their feet, under the town, a pulse that had not stopped when the rats fled. It had felt them. Whatever it was, down there in the dark it had noticed the two who could hear it, and it was afraid, and it was waiting.

*Their adventure had begun. What none of them yet understood was that it had begun long before this night — and that the thing beneath the town had not chosen the two who could hear it at random.*
