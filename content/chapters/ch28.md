# XXVIII. Nesmé
*In which a town rehearses a festival, and the party recognizes the play*

@audio: nesme-lantern fade=8
Three days to the Feast, and @[Nesmé](nesme) stood on its river in the sleet like a town holding two rehearsals at once. Riverwood palisade, watchtowers of gray timber, the River Surbrin running iron-dark under the bridges — a hard moor town of the old style, braced against winter and wolves as its grandmothers had taught it — and everywhere the party looked, the two preparations ran interleaved: candles and crossbow bolts coming off the same wagons; garlands going up the lamp-posts while new-hired blades walked the wall-tops; the Feast of the Moon, when the North sets its table for the dead, being laid in a town that was also, without knowing it, being set as a table. They had come to Nesmé because it was where everything pointed at once — the mark's last word, the sword's cooled certainty, the moor's only warm walls for a day's walk in any direction, and a town paying honest wages against the sleet — and yet something about the doubled wagons sat wrong in eight separate stomachs before anyone could have said why. The year had taught them to file that feeling rather than argue with it. They filed it, and went in through the gate.

Getting inside the town's works cost nothing but a signature, because the town was buying. Blade-coin, as the drovers had said — Nesmé's captains hiring anything that could hold a wall — and eight capable strangers and an orc were signed, billeted — given quarters on the wall's coin — and issued watch-schedules before their boots dried. It was cover so perfect the party distrusted it on principle, and it held anyway: by the first evening they stood posted on the river wall of the town they had come to save, being paid a wage by it, with every gate and roster in the place open to men and women wearing its colors.

@[Saeron](saeron) began stopping in lanes on the first full day. The party noticed — they were, by now, connoisseurs of one another's silences — and said nothing, and followed him at a distance while he read the town the way Berra had once read gauges. The billet-charts posted by quarter, with wardens newly appointed over each. The “voluntary” registry at the market cross, where households wrote their names and numbers for winter provisioning, in a ledger nobody remembered ordering. Lantern-codes chalked discreetly on corner-posts. Certain cellars requisitioned for “flood stores” that stood above the flood line. And the graveyard fence — in a town where the wall itself wanted work — repaired first, new pales bright against the old, the gate rehung to swing outward. On the second evening he stood at the market cross with the registry in his sight, and took the folded page out of his breast pocket, and unfolded it for the first time in front of them all, and laid it against the town, and the two matched like a stencil. “I have seen a town prepared like this before,” Saeron said. Nine years of silence, and then, quite evenly: “@[Iriaebor](iriaebor). The quarter I grew up in. It emptied on a festival night, and the world agreed to call it a plague.” He folded the page along its worn creases. “I will tell the rest at the right fire. But I wanted you to know that I know this play. We have arrived before the curtain.”

Suspicion came first, and it came from a soulless man's parting advice: quieter coin, for quieter work. So the party listened on the walls, where hired blades talk, and heard the shape of an absence: men in this town drawing pay no roster accounted for, standing no post anyone could name — flush men, incurious men, men who did not drink where the wall-watch drank, were on no rotation, and were, once you began looking for them, nowhere at all. Looking for them became the party's second, unsalaried employment. @[Tirig](tirig) spent three evenings as somebody forgettable — a drover's wife, a candle-seller's cousin, a face that slid off the memory like sleet off oilskin — walking behind flush men through the river lanes and losing them, every single time, within a street of the same blind-walled block. @[Corvyn](corvyn) spent one profitable afternoon at the paymasters' table being charming about nothing at all until a second ledger came out from under the first, the way second ledgers do. And @[Amyl](amyl), who had spent a season learning to read sickness through his boot-soles, marked the men who came up from the river stairs at odd bells with a cold on them the weather could not explain — a cold they carried indoors, like a smell.

@audio: mirror-glass fade=4
The quiet coin led them to the wool-house. The ledger gave up its addresses; the shadowing gave up its hours; and what they pointed to, together, from two directions that had never consulted each other, was a riverside wool-warehouse with its cistern drained, its windows bricked, and a cold coming up its stairs that had nothing to do with the season. They did not force it that night. They took something better: the satchel of working-papers its foreman fetched away each dawn, lifted whole between the warehouse and his lodgings by a gnome with opinions about fine print.

The papers were @[Verity](the-harvest)'s. The party knew the hand — the folded page's hand, the amphitheater's hand: square, devout, unhurried — orders of watch and gathering-schedule and lantern-code, the whole liturgy of preparation they had walked through in Triboar's last days, laid out for Nesmé with an enforcer's thoroughness. And threaded through the orders, in a @[second hand](the-editor), small and even and gray as good ink gets, ran the corrections. Not the margins of a rival. The margins of an editor. Against Verity's chosen gathering-square: Not the well square. The market. Grief needs room to walk, and the well narrows the lines of retreat — forgive the intrusion. Against a tally of the quiet crew: Forty is enough. More is appetite, not need. Against a schedule of the night itself, one word moved and initialed by nobody: earlier. The party read the satchel through twice by shuttered lantern, and it was @[Aelendir](aelendir) who said the thing they were all circling, in the careful voice of a man identifying a new instrument by its sound alone: “The plan has an editor. And the editor is better at this than the planner, and is sorry about it, and is right.”

Two things more were in the satchel, and the record sets them down exactly. On the last page of the orders, beneath the final schedule, the second hand had written a line that corrected nothing — a private line, the sort a person writes to themselves at the end of a long night's work: They are being delivered, not taken. (Say it until it is true.) And caught in the seam of the satchel, where a thing slips when its owner rises too quickly: a short cord of gray silk, tied in a small, symmetrical knot. @[Sov](sov) took it out of the seam and went very still. The party watched him turn it once in the lantern light. It was a mourning-knot — the common grief-knot of the northern funerary treatises, tied at ten thousand ordinary funerals — and he knew its name and its diagram because he had stared at that exact knot for a long time once, in a quiet wing of a Vault, on a page that a black feather had been laid in like a bookmark, and he had learned nothing that day, and kept the feather. He did not tell the others how he knew it. He put the cord in his breast pocket, next to where the feathers lived, with the deliberate care of a man filing a letter he has decided not to answer yet.

@[Darz Helgar](darz) was at the graveyard fence, and the party's minds refused it for a full three seconds. The fence. The sleet. The hat held to his chest. A man they had left half-broken beside a shrine in another life, standing at a moor town's graves four hundred miles north of anywhere he had reason to be — and then @[Amyl](amyl) reached him first, and the reunion happened the way reunions happen on roads like theirs: an embrace, a held breath, and then every question at once, tumbling over each other. How. When. Why here, of every here. His road-answers were plain as planking: he had walked; it had taken the autumn; he had worked town to town up the winter roads for beds and bread. It was the other answers that were not answers. Because he had changed, and the party assembled the change in pieces across the hour rather than all at once: the bare hands in the sleet, unremarked by their owner; a gray cast at the rim of each iris that had not been there at the cabin; a steadiness that was new, and welcome, and wrong, like a picture hung one degree off level. And when @[Iratius](iratius), who does not do pieces, finally asked him straight — what happened to you — Darz looked at his own bare hands for a while, with mild interest, as though they were a neighbor's, and said, “Winter and I came to an arrangement,” and that was every answer anyone got, then or ever after. The record has its suspicions, and is keeping them.

What he had instead of answers was a complaint, and he offered it the way countrymen offer weather. An ache. In his teeth, he said — though not in any tooth — worse in some towns than others, and bad enough here that there was one street by the river he had stopped using altogether, because standing on it felt like biting down on metal. He had no name for it and did not pretend to one. He knew only that the quiet towns had stopped feeling quiet to him — “The quiet ones feel like the cabin used to feel. I didn't have a word for it then, either.” — and that he had drifted the way a man drifts after a sound he cannot stop hearing, and that the drifting had run out at @[Nesmé](nesme), where it was loudest. He did not know what he was hearing. He had never been told what had stood humming beside his cabin for two years, nor what his town had actually died of; he had come out of Triboar's fall a punished man with no sentence read to him. So the party read it. That night, gently, all of it — the stone, the pattern, the prepared towns, what his teeth had been counting all winter — and watched a year of senseless grief rearrange itself, line by line, into an enemy with a shape. @[Darz](darz) Helgar was quiet a long time when they finished. “Then I'm glad I walked,” he said at last. “I would rather be a man who knows.” And in the morning he took them through Nesmé street by street, deliberately, mapping where the ache lived; and the worst street, the one his boots refused, ran along the blind brick flank of a riverside wool-warehouse. The party had bought the same address two nights earlier from a paymaster's second ledger. They let his teeth confirm it unprompted — confirmation is worth more that way — and told him so after, and he looked at the warehouse wall for a while and put his hat on.

They had been in Nesmé longer than the party had — that is the correction the record owes this meeting, because it wrote arrivals in its notes and the notes were wrong; some people are simply present, in plain sight, until the story turns its head. The town's Ilmater shrine is a small one — a roadside god-house by the south gate, six kneelers and a rail — and @[Saeron](saeron) went to it at dawn on the third day, as his mornings go. He stopped in the doorway. On the rail, knotted at the precise interval his own hands kept every dawn of this book, hung a second set of crimson cords. The man they belonged to knelt beneath them, travel-worn and broad and kind in the stern way of their order, and he did not need to turn around. “Hello, Saeron,” he said, in the voice of a man greeting family — because, the record will let the door open only this far tonight, that is what he was. Six winters, he said later, at the rail, he had been tracing a pattern of festival-night disappearances up and down the river-roads; prayers and paperwork; and the pattern's arithmetic had pointed him, this winter, here. And the woman rising from the shadowed end of the rail — moor-dressed, work-worn, with the stillness of someone who has told a true story for years to people who filed it under grief — stopped hearing anything at all when she saw the face in the doorway. “You,” @[Bettany](bettany-and-osric) said. Not loud. The word had waited nine years and did not need volume. “The night the quarter burned. You were on the barracks stair. I have been trying for nine years to decide what I saw you do.” The sleet ticked on the shrine roof. Saeron did not look away — he had learned that much from somebody — and gave her the only coin he had that was honest: “So have I.”

The Harper bird found the wall-watch at dusk the same day, iced and furious as ever, and the packet it carried rearranged the eve. @[Aevren](aevren)'s decode — the @[Silent Ledger](the-silent-ledger), opened at last: not whole, she wrote, but walking — and what walked out of the cipher was worse for its shape than for any line of it. The Ledger kept an order's accounts, and the order never once, anywhere in its own pages, wrote its own name — which Aevren confessed frightened her more than any name could have. What it wrote instead was a calendar. The veil-thin nights, festival by festival, entered like quarter-days in a countinghouse: Highharvestide — entered, and reconciled. The Feast of the Moon — entered, and pending. A cadence, not a map; no town was named anywhere; but the party's own eyes had already named the town, and a pending entry three days out needed no address. At the foot of the pending column stood one line of freight-language that @[Saeron](saeron) read aloud once and nobody asked him to repeat: Cargo: as the last intake, in full, less the error. They did not know what the error had been. The record does, and is keeping it for the page where it pays.

And threaded through the accounts, season upon season, ran a counterparty — payments in, product out — signed not with any title but with a true name, in a hand Aevren's note described with unusual care: a fiend's hand, old-fashioned, vain of its flourishes. I cannot place the name and I do not love that I cannot. Do not speak it aloud until we know its weight. — A. It was not the name the mark had given them on the moor. @[Iratius](iratius) made the lamp wait while he checked the flourishes twice against his memory of a spoken word, and it held: a different name entirely — longer, older, vainer of itself. Either the web had two spiders, or one spider wore two names, and nobody at the table found either thought an improvement. They put the page away unspoken, as instructed, each of them privately certain it would not stay unspoken long.

We walked him as far as @[Silverymoon](silverymoon) under two good cloaks. East of it, my lines lost him. I am sorry. I am not, in truth, certain sorry is the right entry. — A.

The same packet carried a second letter, in a hand that slanted like a man writing while walking, and the party read it aloud at the billet table because it was the first purely good news of the winter. @[Theron Veil](theron-veil), from Silverymoon: the Lady is home — he had underlined home twice — and the bridge argues less by the week; whoever had been detuning it has stopped, or fled, or discovered what it is to have a @[Silverhand](alustriel) audit one's work in person. My own survey of the who-profits question proceeds, and has produced a name I will not commit to a bird. The faculty have opinions about my methods. The faculty may file them. There followed three lines of dry professional affection — he addressed the party collectively as my field colleagues, hoped the eladrin was keeping the North's archives honest, and closed with a postscript the party read twice: P.S. The altar-stone hums differently since midwinter. Higher. As though something on the far side of the note had come nearer. Do be careful, colleagues. Things are moving toward their nouns. The party read the postscript twice, and nobody said which noun. The chapel where this record began was a small place very far behind them now, and a hum in one altar-stone was too small a thing to be information for the people they had become — so they took it for what it was instead, which is an omen, and set it aside the way you set aside a letter you know the year intends to answer.

And in the party's own billet, one window outshone the street. @[Aelendir](aelendir) claimed the deep sill on the second evening and began setting candles on it — and kept setting them, going out twice for more, until the sill held a congregation of small flames past easy counting and the glass ran gold with them. He tied each knot himself, unhurried, and named no names aloud. Asked — it was @[Corvyn](corvyn) who finally asked, gently, as one performer to another — for whom the candles burned, Aelendir looked at the crowded sill for a long moment and said, “I ran out of sill,” which was not an answer, and was the whole answer, and nobody asked again. The window burned all three nights. Passers-by in the lane below took to slowing under it, without knowing why, the way people slow past a church.

The days in @[Nesmé](nesme) held small things too, and the record has learned which of those to keep. @[Sov](sov) spent both afternoons on the wall-steps by the market, where the town's children gathered to dress their Feast candles — and taught them, patiently, with his quick pale hands, the proper grief-knot: the small symmetrical tie the old funerary treatises prescribe for a candle carried for the dead, so the flame and the name stay bound. He had learned its diagram in a quiet wing of a Vault, from a page a feather had marked, and he taught it the way @[Saeron](saeron) taught letters — exactly, kindly, without one word of why — and the children, being children, made it a craze, and corrected their parents' knots at supper tables all over town. By Feast eve, half the windows in Nesmé carried Sov Thimron's knots at their candle-bases, small and symmetrical and correct, binding flame to name. He walked the lanes at dusk looking at them with an expression nobody could file, and said only, “They tied them well.”

The council of war met in their billet that night: eight of the company, a monk, a witness, and a slightly unmoored man who could hear the enemy curing. The shape of it settled fast, because the year had trained them all for exactly this table. Warning the town openly had been tried once, in another town, by better-placed people, and had lost to management; here they held something Triboar never had — position, wages, wall-colors, and foreknowledge with dates on it. The working could not fire before the Feast — the veil-night was its engine; Iriaebor had taught its one unwilling scholar that much. What exactly waited under the wool-house, @[Saeron](saeron) would not assert; he laid the evidence in a row instead, like a man dealing cards face up: a festival three days out. A drained cistern. A cold on the stairs no season made. A street a changed man's teeth refused. And nine years ago, in the only other prepared town anyone at this table had survived, the instrument at the bottom of everything had been a mirror of @[black glass](the-glass), mid-working, that one fire had broken. “I will not swear it is the same,” he said. “I will bet the town that it is.” Which, that night, was the same thing — and it gave the plan its whole shape: whatever was curing down there had to survive until moonrise on the third night, and therefore, very simply, it must not. Break the glass before the moon, and the Harvest of Nesmé dies in its cradle. They built the plan in low voices around @[Darz](darz)'s map of the night-work — approaches, hours, the two men who carried nothing — and the record will keep the plan's details for the chapter that spends them. What it will keep here is the end of the evening: @[Osric](bettany-and-osric) the monk looking around the crowded little room at the company his brother kept, at the orc practicing letters in the candle-smoke and the gnome annotating a stolen satchel and the woman no frost would touch, and saying, in the tone of a professional assessing a professional matter, “Six winters I have chased this pattern with prayers and paperwork. You people intend to simply go and break it.” And @[Gorvach](gorvach), not looking up from the shield where he was carving his second word, answered for the house: “Yes.”

The last two days ran down like sand. Candles went into windows all over Nesmé — the Feast's custom, one flame for each beloved dead, so that by the second evening the whole town glimmered like a congregation of small vigils, a town rehearsing its dead at exactly the wrong scale — and the party stood their bought watches and walked their bought rounds and did not sleep much. @[Saeron](saeron) spent an hour of the last afternoon at the graveyard fence, at one end of it, with @[Bettany](bettany-and-osric) at the other, neither speaking, both staying; whatever was to be decided between them had agreed to wait for the fire he had promised. And the moon came up over the Evermoors on the third evening fat and white and early — earlier, said a small even hand in a stolen satchel, and the party alone in all that candlelit town knew to find the word terrifying — and the Feast of the Moon came up the river with the dusk, and the doors of the year swung toward their last night.
